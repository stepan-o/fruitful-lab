## Audience contract

The governing lens is the economics of creative work and the audience’s
attachment to it. Use attachment for what a work comes to mean to people; use
trust for confidence in the next offer, and engagement/retention only for
specified observed behaviors. None proves the others. Keep this distinction
through later chapters on social life, calendars, identity and measurement.

The opening now illustrates cinema admissions versus a subscription catalog,
using Netflix’s own [July 2024 shareholder letter](https://ir.netflix.net/files/doc_financials/2024/q2/FINAL-Q2-24-Shareholder-Letter.pdf).
The [2023 WGA agreement summary](https://www.wgacontract2023.org/the-campaign/summary-of-the-2023-wga-mba)
supports a specific compensation change: a bonus for qualifying streaming
productions tied to audience reach, with confidential data access for the Guild.
Do not turn it into an unsupported claim that streaming alone caused every
change in production, employment or writing quality. These are historical
sources, not a statement of current employment terms.

Assume an interested adult, possibly an experienced player, but no required
knowledge of Diablo or games. Preserve the depth of the industry argument.
Introduce the relevant game, organization or system where it first does work
in that argument. This supersedes the earlier assumption that readers know D2.

An introduction should establish what something is, what someone does with it,
and why it matters here. Prefer a useful clause to a glossary interruption:

- Diablo: Blizzard Entertainment's dark-fantasy game series, in which players
  guide a character through combat, collect equipment and develop its abilities.
- Class: a type of character with its own abilities and ways of fighting.
- Build: the particular combination of abilities and equipment a player chooses.
- Campaign: the game's main story adventure. An act is a major part of it.
- Loot: equipment and other items found through play; a drop is an item a
  defeated enemy or opened container yields.
- Core loop: the sequence of actions and rewards that repeatedly carries play
  forward. Show Diablo's fighting, finding equipment and growing stronger before
  applying the term.
- Live service: a game operated and updated over time. Explain its actual
  payment model separately; the term does not itself mean a subscription.
- Cosmetics: purchases that change appearance rather than combat capabilities.

Explain experience points, levels, seasons, expansions and paid reward systems
when their rules become necessary. Introduce a studio through its relevant
work; identify publishers and storefronts by their role in the example. Avoid
adding a corporate biography to every name. Where the same company makes and
publishes a game, do not force an artificial separation.

Each chapter can be opened directly. Give its central example enough local
orientation to stand alone, without repeating the full introduction. Captions
and diagram labels need the same treatment as prose. Definitions essential to
understanding must remain visible; an optional glossary can hold extra detail.
Expand abbreviations on first use, or use ordinary language when the abbreviation
buys us nothing. Avoid explaining one unfamiliar term with three more.

## The wider argument

Diablo gives us a case in which recognizable play survives substantial changes
to the surrounding product. Keep four questions distinct:

1. What does the player do, and what makes doing it worthwhile?
2. How is a playthrough organized, and what supports another attempt?
3. What is included in a purchase, and when can the player be asked to pay again?
4. What continuing work, infrastructure and social participation sustain it?

The broader economic question is how continuing enjoyment becomes a continuing
commercial relationship, and what that relationship changes. This is our
synthesis, not a claim that every game, service or decade follows one path.
Repeat use, repeat spending, digital distribution and ongoing operation remain
separate dimensions. D2 already offered online play and rich replayability.

a succession of unrelated metaphors through a paragraph. Follow the comparison
back into the concrete game case.

## Economic and social context to weave into existing chapters

**Distribution and operation.** Goldfarb and Tucker's *Digital Economics*
organizes research around falling search, replication, transport, tracking and
verification costs. That gives us a basis for explaining why reaching players,
distributing updates and maintaining a measurable relationship became easier.
It does not say that creative production, support or servers became free, or
that a particular game had to adopt a particular model.
[Research abstract, 2017 working paper / 2019 journal article](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23684).

**A changed purchase relationship.** Adobe's FY2013 report documents its move
to deliver new creative features through Creative Cloud, with CS6 the last major
perpetual-license release. Use this as a parallel to changes around a familiar
activity, not a template for D4's pricing or proof of its developer's motives.
[Adobe FY2013 annual report, printed p. 39](https://www.adobe.com/content/dam/cc/en/investor-relations/pdfs/ADBE-10K-FY13-FINAL.pdf).

**Changes do not happen on one axis.** Netflix added streaming in January 2007
to an existing DVD subscription. Its announcement explicitly distinguishes
rental, download-to-own and advertising-supported delivery. This is a useful
historical counterexample to a simplistic physical-product-to-subscription
timeline: delivery can change while payment structure persists.
[Netflix's original announcement](https://about.netflix.com/en/news/netflix-offers-subscribers-the-option-of-instantly-watching-movies-on-their).

**Leisure as a social setting.** Steinkuehler and Williams' 2006 work on online
games as third places draws on studies of Lineage and Asheron's Call. It supports
treating some online worlds as settings for informal sociability. Introduce
this alongside the friends in our existing example. It is not evidence that all
games provide the same community, or that more time online means greater welfare.
[Original research](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1083-6101.2006.00300.x).

**Identity and consumer culture.** Lehdonvirta's 2009 work examines functional,
hedonic and social attributes in virtual-item purchase decisions. Our wardrobe
and souvenir comparisons can make those motives intelligible without assuming
that digital objects are either worthless or inherently worth their price.
[Author's research record](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1351769).

**Products that can observe and respond.** Microsoft's 2009 experimentation
paper describes evaluating changes to websites and services through controlled
experiments. It provides a cross-industry bridge to the essay's evidence and
measurement chapters. Our question is what teams choose to improve, and whether
their measures capture what players value. Measurement is not itself evidence
of manipulation; this source establishes no particular Blizzard testing practice.
[Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/online-experimentation-at-microsoft/).

**Culture and continuity.** Long-running characters and settings can acquire
personal and shared history, as they do in film and book series. D4's anniversary
return to Tristram is the concrete case already sourced in the opening. Develop
how familiar fiction meets new commercial arrangements without claiming to
measure nostalgia or treating every returning player as nostalgic.

Present the 1990s–2020s as overlapping histories of products, delivery, services,
community and commerce. The Diablo series covers roughly thirty of those years.
Avoid a universal progression from good complete boxes to bad incomplete
services, or a claim that services inevitably improve older games. Explain
both the work they can support and the demands they can place on people's money,
time and relationships. Funding incentives influence design without explaining
every design decision.
