# Writing style

In the studio dialogue, bob sees the first cortex and says, “One.” In the approved script he says, “I made that.” It is the first thing he has made here. He has good reason to look at it for a second. STILETTO has good reason to want him facing the other way. Her “Shut up and keep the line moving” cuts his pleasure short, but the player has already had a second to share it. That second is worth writing for.

### What each draft brought to the gate

The October studio cut gave the day its machinery. Ten workers arrive. The checkpoint holds up production. One worker, then two, then a crew of eight make three cortexes. ed and gus spend the shift at Security. The performed dialogue opens with bob asking, “Is this the right queue?” sam tells him to stay close. That introduces a newcomer and a friend. The first rewrite found something the workers might actually be doing while they wait. max tries out LIMEN’s welcome, jo corrects a word, and bob leans in to see how max made the sound. The approved cut keeps that exchange. When the real LIMEN says “privilege,” jo’s six years at this gate become audible in a single word.

STILETTO walks through the gate without showing ID in all three versions. Listen to how long bob has to stand there after he notices.

| Version | What happens to bob’s objection |
| --- | --- |
| Studio dialogue | “She didn’t show you a card.” LIMEN demands that he speak up. bob saves himself with “Just admiring the Protocol, sir. Very thorough.” There is a punch line, then an assignment check. |
| First rewrite | “Would you like me to call her back?” gives LIMEN a better way to frighten him. The scene keeps going. bob says “No, sir,” LIMEN offers to bring her into the conversation again, and bob explains that a man at the gate must know everyone by sight. We have already seen him retreat before he starts explaining. |
| Approved cut | “You didn’t ask for her ID.” bob has noticed exactly what LIMEN did. “Would you like me to call her back?” LIMEN lets him through. bob can take the opening or keep arguing. We see his answer when he goes. |

LIMEN can sound courteous here because he controls the gate. Save his full-volume voice for the moment he needs a whole queue to answer. With STILETTO, listen for what she needs done before choosing how loudly she says it. “Good. Now wash it.” is a perfectly good STILETTO line. By the fourth check she has seen how many workers are missing, and “MANURE-FILLED ROBOT BRAINS” comes out at the top of her lungs. Even at her angriest she is still thinking about what she could put toward the quota.

### What the middle drafts taught us to cut

The first rewrite gave sam a hand-squeeze gag that ended, “Christ, bob. Leave me the use of a finger.” It made us wait for a laugh while ed was picking up the card. In the final cut sam kneels beside bob, ed stops the skidding card with his boot and pockets it, and STILETTO needs the gate held. All three get to act. bob has every reason to miss what ed did. Much later, ed clips his pen into the pocket with the card. Nobody has to remind the audience whose card it is.

A later Wash pass stopped on a bead of black seeping back through the folds and sent the brain through a second rinse. It promised a fault worth investigating. Nothing later in the shift depended on that fault. The final wash runs clear and he moves on. The bad knee earns a longer shot. It gives halfway through the loader stroke, so bob sits on the motor housing and finishes the pull. STILETTO looks past him at the seated brain. From their two different concerns we learn how the loader works, how badly he was hurt, and what counts as good work on her floor.

The studio had STILETTO call out “bob: Intake and Belt. sam: Wash and Forge.” A working rewrite let bob warn her about the press handle. The approved exchange earns her one use of a worker’s name. The door sticks. She tells bob to hit it. He wants to know how she knows. “I’ve stood at this fucking press, bob.” For one beat bob learns that she used to stand where he stands. The next worker can still be “You there.” sam’s heavy tray-carrying frame in the original Conveyor painting tells us where he belongs.

### How to use the references in a rewrite

**Back to the Dawn gives the count its timing.** The studio already had LIMEN’s call, lou’s mutter and max’s answer. A working pass had gus explain how well his voice carried. Let his volume audition for the job. Hold the room when LIMEN asks who really cares. lou mutters “noone...” to ben while max calls “you, sir!” gus’s “I DO!” comes in over max. ed already has his pen out when he asks about a fresh page. Rehearse all four against the same cue. Layer their entrances and leave the bubbles up long enough to read. Four tidy turns would give the workers time to consider each other’s answers. The pleasure here is hearing where each man’s mind went in the same instant. [Morning-count frame and study](/loopforge-design/narrative-reference.html)

**The Tarantino dialogue study gives the gate its interruption.** In the stagecoach discussion, a new arrival changes the company for everyone already aboard. Try the welcome from both sides of that entrance. Let max enjoy the impression, jo enjoy correcting him and bob enjoy listening. Then let the person being imitated walk into earshot. Later, read the ID exchange aloud from bob’s spot, with STILETTO close enough to call back. The actor playing LIMEN has already made the offer dangerous. That is where to cut bob’s polite reply. [Dialogue and the arrival of a new speaker](/loopforge-design/narrative-dialogue.html)

**Astafiev and the original paintings give a body something to do.** In the opening of *Cursed and Killed*, a recruit moves his cap away from his ear as a distant sound begins to resolve into singing. The gesture lets us discover what he hears. max’s jaw clicks behind his ear. bob wants to know how he did it. When everybody else turns back to LIMEN, bob is still watching max. At Intake, make the writer place sam’s loaded tray on the rails. At Security, place ed’s boot between the loose card and the grate. A writer who can point to the hand, load or sightline can usually give the actor a better direction than “he looks nervous.” [Astafiev and the felt world](/loopforge-design/narrative-astafiev.html) · [The two storyboard paintings](/stepanoskin/loopforge/design#day-one-intro)

**Dostoevsky gives ed room to surprise us.** Marmeladov still has the manners of an official when his clothes tell a very different story about his standing. Give ed the same courtesy of a second look. He pockets another worker’s card, volunteers to keep the register, and takes pride in accounting for all eight. At the bell LIMEN walks past his outstretched register. Outside, ed is the first to laugh at max. Keep all four actions. An actor who plays ed as the designated informer all day will find the laugh hard to give. He can genuinely enjoy the impression with bob’s card still in his pocket. [Character introduction and official manners](/loopforge-design/narrative-studies.html)

### How the final sequence moves

Cut back to Security when STILETTO runs out of hands. Return to Conveyor when the next clearance changes what the crew can do. Those two rooms give the editor a reason for every trip across the corridor.

Spend time on the first brain. bob has to get it through Intake, Wash, Belt and Forge on his bad leg. On the second, sam can load the next tray while bob works. On the third, follow one brain down the staffed line in a continuous shot. lou says “Three.” The camera has already shown whose work it was. At Security the same economy runs the other way. Give max’s fourth check time for STILETTO’s threat. Then four stamps take mae, kit, lou and ben through. Repetition has taught the audience enough to let the cut accelerate.

The studio ended with bob and sam leaving together. The first rewrite had gus shut a door so max could ask, “May I have the door?” ed objected to the impression and jo explained which part max had got right. The approved ending needs only a short walk. max borrows ed’s “All eight accounted for, sir.” gus asks, “Do LIMEN.” The jaw goes back into the collar and the welcome voice from the first shot comes out hollering the count. ed laughs first. Behind him the lights go out. Give the laugh and the cut to black the last word.

### Before a scene goes to rehearsal

Read it once as the least powerful person present. Mark the first moment he can leave, get back to work or keep his mouth shut. Then run it with the voices muted. Follow the injured leg, the card, the register and a single brain. Then listen for what the silent pass lost. jo knows the exact word of the welcome. bob wants to know how the jaw works. gus wants the impression again. Put those voices back. Keep worker names lowercase and supervisors uppercase. Use the pronouns established for each person in the scene. A private aside needs one listener. The whole queue gets to answer only when someone actually calls to it.
