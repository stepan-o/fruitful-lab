Writing Loopforge

Editorial brief · Revision 4 · 20 October 2026

After LIMEN strikes bob, the queue becomes easier to manage. Nobody moves without being told. bob gets up, tests the leg and goes back to waiting. A successful inspection can look like this.

The writing should stay with bob long enough for us to feel the effort of that return. It should also notice the worker who makes room and the one who watches LIMEN instead. Their conduct gives the scene its life. Calling it a demonstration of authority tells us very little about being there.

The voice

Loopforge needs a voice with affection for its people and a sharp ear for the language used to govern them. It can be rough, funny, sensuous and angry. Its judgments should arise from a close acquaintance with the place: how a shift wears on a body, how a reprimand changes a room, how good it feels to be wanted by somebody who has no work to give you.

The Astafiev study is our reference for that closeness. His opening gives the setting a bodily existence and the narrator a moral presence. The long sentences matter: sensation accumulates, understanding is delayed. Reducing this to “use concrete details” misses the writing itself.

In Loopforge, the machine body gives familiar experiences unfamiliar consequences. A worn joint can be repaired, but asking for the repair means admitting wear to someone who decides whether you are worth keeping. Words such as serviceable, spare and replacement belong in the workshop; familiarity with their use gives them force when they enter a conversation about a person. We should not have to explain the metaphor.

The factory must also be somewhere people live. They waste time together, show off, get bored, enjoy doing something well. A joke can be stupid and still be welcome. Let a good afternoon remain a good afternoon, without turning every pleasure into advance notice of disaster. bob needs a life the player enjoys returning to.

On the page and on the screen

Give a passage enough time to develop. A long sentence can carry a gathering disturbance; a plain sentence can let its consequence settle. Avoid the repeated snap of declarations, the solemn fragment, the neat moral after every exchange. Read aloud for a speaking voice, not a succession of captions.

Dialogue should have an immediate listener and a reason to be spoken. The characters can misunderstand themselves, lose their temper, tell a poor lie or ask for something they cannot quite name. Their language comes from their circumstances. LIMEN can call coercion a precaution with complete sincerity. STILETTO can object to an injured worker being held at Security because she needs that worker on the line. The objection does not make her kind.

Prose can take us inside an experience. In the game, we have to find its visible and audible life: a hand offered and left waiting, a worker testing the floor before committing weight, a conversation that stops when a supervisor approaches. Leave room to notice these things. Speech bubbles should let people speak to one another; they should not tell the audience what to feel.

The design essays owe the reader the same care. Explain the situation, follow the difficulty and arrive at a decision. Use ordinary language where it is exact. Cut repeated principles and phrases that promise an experience the passage has not supplied. Sanctuary’s opening chapters remain our reference for developing an argument without losing the reader.

Keep bob at the center as the factory grows. Workers retain their lowercase names; supervisors retain their capitals. The writing has done its first job when “STILETTO hit bob” recalls someone the player knows.
