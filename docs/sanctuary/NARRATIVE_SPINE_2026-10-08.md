## Argument and evidence boundaries

Chapter 2 follows one creative work sustaining several businesses, from Sony’s console economics to Microsoft’s acquired franchises and NVIDIA’s computing. Subscription is the appealing public offer through which an operator can earn from an ongoing relationship with games. CD PROJEKT’s catalog agreement makes that relationship concrete: fewer individual purchases can coexist with a better return. The close moves the transaction inside play, where Candy Crush can offer assistance once its challenge already matters to the player. This is a conceptual transition, not a chronology in which catalog subscriptions caused mobile freemium.

Chapter 3 asks what mobile freemium did to the economics of creative work. King’s browser tournaments establish its original roles as maker, publisher and portal operator. Small-team experimentation and the Saga format lead into free entry and optional assistance. The point is the next sale inside an existing work: challenge and progress can support both enjoyment and an offer. King’s buyer’s-remorse principle identifies the tension; its continuing maintenance burden shows the work required to sustain the relationship. The acquisition sequence answers who owns King now, while the contrast with Diablo distinguishes assistance from cosmetics and paid expansions. Distribution costs lead into Valve in chapter 4. This is not an inventory of player-visible features followed by business facts, and does not attribute direct design borrowing without evidence.

The companies share late-1990s game milestones, not identical founding dates or exclusive business models. Valve still makes games; Epic operates games as well as tools and distribution; Rockstar combines authored releases and continuing online production. Their next customers and commitments differ.

Diablo is the continuous historical thread: inherited role-playing ideas become immediate action; the purchased adventure supports repeated play; D2 expands the journey and later formalizes seasonal ladder competition; D3 exposes a conflict between obtaining equipment and enjoying the hunt; D4 combines a campaign with a continuing production calendar. Gauntlet then shows that selling continued life is older than these worlds. Replayability never serves as a synonym for recurring revenue.

The final acts follow what the player is trying to do, how rules structure that activity, what purchases change, and what evidence would distinguish a successful offer from displacement, pressure or friction. Public sources establish mechanisms and documented decisions. Illustrative instruments are labeled models, not estimates of private performance. Dated offers and screenshots do not promise present catalog availability.

New original history scenes and comparison controls share the established stone, brass, ember and muted teal palette. Motion is CSS based and pauses offscreen, on hidden pages, under the global motion preference and with reduced motion. Source images use responsive, locally hosted derivatives; rights records state the source, owner, analytical purpose and limitations without claiming blanket permission.

The production/engine chapter includes Loopforge using the project’s own assets. Original art and primary-source visual citations serve specific arguments rather than decorating chapter boundaries.

## The mobile bridge

The ecosystem overview introduces three acquired audiences with official brand art, keeping the makers visible. King explains how the next purchase can emerge inside a work people already enjoy, and why earning again requires preserving that relationship. Its platform bill makes Valve’s move into running a store a concrete next question. Mobile is a business model case, not a claim that casual games are simple or that Diablo copied King. The new chapter uses a complete continuous manuscript and preserves the approved Pong opening.
