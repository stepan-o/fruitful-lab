Animator — second studio interview

Fresh answers for the owner-approved R10 milestone.

Q1

What is the most useful result you personally contributed this cycle? Describe the player/team outcome, what you actually inspected, and the evidence that could contradict your assessment.

My most useful contribution was turning the recorded Day1 into a shared, addressable review. I extracted 261 frames in nine bounded operations and inspected selected sequences. Director, Level and Engine could refer to the same video offsets instead of discussing different impressions of “the walk.” The clearest example is a departing worker, probably Max, whose arms remain spread while nearby workers lower theirs. That narrows the correction to an actor and bank without pretending I know the cause. A replay identifying another actor, or showing a different selected cue, would change that attribution. The packet helps the team fix the game; it is not itself a repaired performance.

Q2

Which decision of your own worked poorly? Give one concrete instance, its consequence, what you knew then, and what you would change. Separate your responsibility from dependencies outside your control.

I started the first complete Sam action with an unverified joint name, Spine2. The actual rig did not contain it. The run stopped after about three seconds with no authored frames or contact evidence. My wrapper also returned success despite the failed child process. Those were my errors, not a browser-access problem or evidence that the body could not perform the action. I had frozen inputs and could have checked every required joint and both wrapper exit paths before using the slot. Producer had to send the packet back. I would make those small preflight checks part of preparing the action, rather than count a runnable command as readiness.

Q3

Describe one cross-role exchange that improved the game and one that created avoidable delay or rework. What made the difference? Name the work and evidence, not just the participants.

Director's release-before-rise instruction improved the opening handoff. I supplied the complete 2.4-second Sam release at rate 1.2, and Engine's integrated source places it before Bob's recovery while keeping STILETTO's arrival during recovery. The inspected frames show Sam helping and Ed taking the card as distinct intentions. Continuous contact is still unverified, but the sequence has a specific causal order to preserve.

The avoidable exchange was the R2 return for rig binding and truthful exit reporting. Producer's correction was useful, yet it consumed coordination that my own preflight should have prevented. The difference was concrete shared timing and ownership in the first exchange, versus an unchecked assumption passed downstream in the second.

Q4

What did actual integrated footage or play reveal that source checks or isolated craft work did not? If you have not inspected moving evidence, say so and distinguish still-frame judgment from motion judgment.

I inspected decoded still sequences, not playable continuous video. They revealed things source checks had not established: the persistent lateral-arm departure, a fallen Bob who reads more like a bent standing figure, speech panels covering faces, and the exit facade masking the action. The Max bank has arm rotation channels; their presence does not establish a good pose. The factory export records production and departures, but that does not prove hand contact with a console. Conversely, a motionless tutorial frame can be an intentional reading hold. I cannot infer foot sliding, precise impact timing or performance causes from these sampling intervals.

Q5

The first retrospective called for one owned outcome, decisive evidence and a change-of-approach decision after a failed assumption. What concrete behavior in this cycle followed or missed that lesson? What should good work in your domain mean now?

The corrected Sam waist run did follow part of the first lesson: it reached a specific failure and stopped. At source 1.2 seconds, the right hand crossed the right upper leg; no candidate bank was exported. That rejected the tested path, not every conceivable storage method. What I missed was making the complete visible action decisive early enough. Several later preparations still made machinery for proving fit before delivering a usable performance. Good animation work now means one recognizable action on the actual body, in the actual scene, with honest custody, support and transitions. A reproducible rejection is useful, but repeated rejections are not progress toward an accepted action by themselves.

Q6

Choose one improvement you would keep for the next cycle. Who owns it, when should it happen, and what observable result would show it helped? Include any tradeoff or disagreement with the current production approach.

I would keep one shared first-action review before producing variants. I own the action and the small rig/exit preflight; Director owns its intended view; Engine provides the matching runtime identity; Producer schedules the review and decides whether the method continues. The observable result is simple: a reviewer can identify the action and transfer without a caption, and the associated contact evidence covers that same run. If it fails, change the method before another bank or detail tier. This delays broad output at first. I prefer that cost to another sequence of source-only packets whose central physical assumption remains untested.

Q7

Which actual character action is convincing, and which clip/pose/contact assumption remains unproven? How did the approved Sam deferral affect useful delivery without erasing the unresolved craft issue?

Ed's pickup intention is the clearest action in the sampled opening: he goes to the dropped card and lifts it while Sam goes to Bob. The player can read two different responses. I would preserve that staging. I cannot certify the exact grip, card continuity between every frame, or Sam's support clearance. Bob's held fall and Max's travelling arm pose also need attention.

The approved Sam deferral let clearance and work participation continue without treating an absent card performance as solved. That was useful scope control. It did not produce a convincing draw-and-return action, and I should not erase that debt by relabelling a listening gesture or status panel as one. The owner-approved R10 slice stands; the deferred precision work remains a separate future decision.
