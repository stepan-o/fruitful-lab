Systems Designer — second interview

Fresh answers, written after my own actual-evidence inspection and before consulting any other new interview answers or Root account.

Q1

What is the most useful result you personally contributed this cycle? Describe the player/team outcome, what you actually inspected, and the evidence that could contradict your assessment.

My most useful contribution was making the later-cohort choice compete with keeping the factory running. The first proposal counted a new visit’s cost, but compared it with no serious alternative. S4 retained jo and max’s learning and showed attendance against postponement from the same prior state. It also let the player arrange kit’s practice without buying another Theatre visit. That gives the team a choice it can fairly stage, rather than a lesson the player must purchase to advance. I inspected the source schedules and cast foundations, then checked the accounting. I have now inspected sampled R10 capture frames and its export, not live play. If viewers understand lou’s later initiative but still never want the visit, the right result is to omit it; correct arithmetic would not defend it.

Q2

Which decision of your own worked poorly? Give one concrete instance, its consequence, what you knew then, and what you would change. Separate your responsibility from dependencies outside your control.

I made the repeated attendance schedule look more justified than it was. On Day 7, kit and lou consumed a whole-line pause, while the only worked output benefit still came from jo and max’s earlier learning. I knew both facts. I should have combined them before sending the passage onward. Critic’s no-visit comparison exposed the omission. Carrying the old cue can make the no-visit A fixture produce seven instead of five, so the opportunity cost is larger than my presentation suggested. The consequence was avoidable revision to already developed scenes and another contract. That was my design omission, not a missing engine feature. I would put the unchanged-state alternative on the page before commissioning the second cohort’s scene.

Q3

Describe one cross-role exchange that improved the game and one that created avoidable delay or rework. What made the difference? Name the work and evidence, not just the participants.

The R8 exchange about staffing improved the game. Level distinguished four actual controls from eight staged duties, and Engine showed why assignment could not be treated as a universal permission list: the approved paired sequence deliberately has bob and sam operate different controls. We kept that behavior, corrected shared-workpiece/stage guards and made occupancy describe bodies rather than assignments. The useful exchange contained a counterexample and a bounded implementation decision. The avoidable rework was my later-cohort recommendation moving into Writer’s C4/D4 continuations before I had shown what the next visit bought. We had well-formed handoffs, but the central design comparison was missing. More complete manifests would not have supplied it.

Q4

What did actual integrated footage or play reveal that source checks or isolated craft work did not? If you have not inspected moving evidence, say so and distinguish still-frame judgment from motion judgment.

I have inspected actual screenshots and ordered extracted frame pages, not continuous footage or a live session. They show a player-facing contradiction that a completed-action count cannot: the clock says End of shift during arrivals, while a status strip says the staffed line is working before the staffed gate. At the Forge gate, the machine is visible but bob is almost below the picture; speech panels cover supervisors’ faces. The exported day still reaches three completions and twelve departures. That makes the distinction concrete: a valid ending does not ensure a truthful explanation along the way. The sampled departure sequence also shows an arms-out silhouette across changing positions, but I cannot turn that into a diagnosis or a smoothness verdict.

Q5

The first retrospective called for one owned outcome, decisive evidence and a change-of-approach decision after a failed assumption. What concrete behavior in this cycle followed or missed that lesson? What should good work in your domain mean now?

S4 followed the lesson when I changed the recommendation instead of defending its premise: later cohorts became optional, the existing learning survived postponement, and I removed the fixed second-session unlock count. The earlier miss was treating source completeness as decisive evidence of a worthwhile repeated day. Good Systems work now means a person can identify the available alternative, what an action costs, who receives its meaning and what changes later. A trace is necessary when those claims concern state or knowledge. A performed comparison is necessary when the claim is that somebody will want the next day. Neither kind of evidence should stand in for the other.

Q6

Choose one improvement you would keep for the next cycle. Who owns it, when should it happen, and what observable result would show it helped? Include any tradeoff or disagreement with the current production approach.

Keep one matched no-addition case before expanding a new mechanic. Systems should own it when the first concrete encounter is drafted, before Writer and Director invest in a full continuation. Producer should carry that pair into the already scheduled integrated review. The observable result is that a reviewer can name a reason to choose either path and point to the later action that actually happened; if there is no reason to buy the addition, we cut or compress it. The tradeoff is fewer early scene variants and sometimes a less polished first presentation. I prefer that to polishing a compulsory visit and discovering later that its strongest incentive is an unlock requirement.

Q7

What did the implemented Day1 teach you about the inherited design and the later factory proposal? How did the cohort/postponement criticism change your judgment rather than merely your documents?

The implemented first shift gives the inherited design a useful physical anchor: fewer people travel between several controls, then a staffed line can distribute the work. That is already a basis for character and authority; it does not need an invented catastrophe to matter. It also exposes the limits of my later proposal. A workpiece count is not a new person, an assignment is not occupation of a control, and a displayed speech is not necessarily heard. The cohort criticism changed my preference, not just my labels: I no longer recommend daily attendance by default. I want a particular return, such as lou carrying kit’s consented request, and I accept that producing or offering practice directly can be better. The inherited conditioning arc remains larger than a handover lesson, but each additional encounter still has to earn its place.
