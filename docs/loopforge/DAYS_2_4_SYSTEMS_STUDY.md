# The second morning at Loopforge

**Game systems research · November 18–19, 2026 · [Build plan for owner approval](DAYS_2_4_SYSTEMS_PLAN.md)**

The owner found the initial designs too narrowly focused on the card. The card is one of several arcs. The proposed opening, controls and Days 2–4 sequence below are unapproved interview history. Existing Day 1 approvals and the source inventory still stand.

**Owner direction from the interview.** Different ways of running the factory can yield different quantities of cortexes. Safety and the satisfaction of protocol, speed and the excitement of daredevil work, deliberate optimization, trying unfamiliar controls and casually looking around should each give a player something meaningful to experience. Choices must show up on the factory floor through movement, machine rhythm, speech and the arcs that follow. These play styles may mix within one run.

**What the historical sources call the product.** The [Puppeteer doctrine](https://github.com/stepan-o/loopforge/blob/3267ea7/docs/legacy/sim2/psych/THE_PUPPETTEER_DOCTRINE.md) follows physical events through individual attribution into daily narratives, recaps and explorable perspectives. Its [companion manifesto](https://github.com/stepan-o/loopforge/blob/3267ea7/docs/legacy/sim2/psych/THE_PUPPETTEER_README.md) describes a forensic story of how a change in conditions propagated through a workplace. The [Producer Vision](https://github.com/stepan-o/loopforge/blob/3267ea7/docs/legacy/sim2/narrative/PRODUCER_VISION.md) judges the result as an episode that can surprise someone and still be explained. For the current game the primary evidence lives on the floor. A player can see or overhear a beat, find its aftermath, or later hear a partial account. The old reporting layer kept emotional shading and long memory interpretive. Current behavioral BDI also lets specific observations and memories change the next action an agent attempts. A recap can interpret the resulting history. It cannot supply a missing cause.

## Owner interview · How Day 1 decisions work

Workers may withhold their own labor or stop the line themselves when they can perform the action. Supervisors can react to an act they perceive, or notice that expected work is failing to happen. Their responses can be unpleasant. Each participant has their own evidence and motives. Day 1 provides a specific performance this model must be able to express. The same relationships among perception, daily desire, persistent intention and action must support other performances on subsequent days.

**Starting points.** The workers arrive disposed to chat with one another, heed supervisors, avoid work and treat the bell as the end of the day. bob is also curious. ed and gus each prefer Security and consider themselves better than the other workers. LIMEN puts protocol first and approaches unfamiliar supervisors cautiously. He wants to punish violations and run tight, individual checks of identity and assignment. STILETTO puts quota first and meets unfamiliar supervisors aggressively. She wants the Conveyor running as soon as possible and at least five cortexes today. These are distinct ambitions. Three finished cortexes can evidence a running line and a missed personal target at the same time.

| Encounter in the approved performance | Decisions the system needs to express |
| --- | --- |
| The first check | LIMEN orders the nearby workers to wait until called. bob hears the first-shift question and takes it as a summons to present his ID. His desire to comply produces an approach. LIMEN sees someone crossing the line despite his order and chooses to strike. Injury, the dropped card and ed taking possession are distinct physical outcomes. sam can attend to bob while ed acts. The workers take away evidence that perceived disobedience brings punishment. |
| Two different clearances | Satisfied that he has enforced the rule, LIMEN judges bob compliant. STILETTO demands passage to get to Conveyor. Supervisors can issue orders to workers and demands to peers. LIMEN's caution toward an unfamiliar peer makes him grant clearance and hold the gate. Seeing the exception, bob asks why her ID went unchecked. LIMEN classifies this as a question and answers sarcastically. Preserve the scripted order on the floor, including STILETTO passing before bob is waved through. |
| One operator and two desks | STILETTO sees one available worker. She has yet to learn what Security is doing. Her comment gets an addressed reply. Her production goal generates successive orders to bob at the four controls. Meanwhile LIMEN calls individuals, verifies them and clears them to follow their initial Conveyor assignments. His call for helpers invites simultaneous, differently motivated responses. ed and gus have the strongest reasons to take the two desk assignments. max can call out and lou can mutter to ben while those jobs are awarded. |
| A change of pace | Elapsed time, available hands and jo’s report give STILETTO grounds to seek more workers. She goes to Security and demands faster clearance. LIMEN can comply with a peer's demand out of caution, finish max's check and speed subsequent checks. Workers hear the faster instructions and move accordingly. STILETTO assigns the arriving crew to stations and orders a staffed cycle. Her judgment that LIMEN is ridiculously slow can become a lasting belief overnight. |
| The bell | The factory clock rings with three cortexes on the rack. The workers' preference to leave and their understanding of the bell outweigh the pull to obey STILETTO's attempt to keep them. They stop, answer differently, and leave. STILETTO can observe the loss of her crew, end the shift and follow. Keep the approved timing of her final line and the slow supervisor exit. Her five-cortex target remains unsatisfied. The workers' social desires also support the scripted conversations, asides and imitations the camera can follow during work and departure. |

**Daytime response and overnight change.** On Day 1 bob can misread a summons, LIMEN can revise his judgment of bob, and STILETTO can respond to a missing crew immediately. Observations start accumulating with the first interaction. Lasting beliefs update overnight. The Day 1 group experiences the consequence of crossing LIMEN and carries a strengthened default desire to heed supervisors into Day 2. STILETTO can start Day 2 already expecting a delay from LIMEN. The relative force of several desires still depends on the situation. Exact representation and scoring are open.

**Inspectable arcs.** A useful trace would preserve the words spoken, intended audience, what an individual heard or saw, their interpretation, the desires in conflict, what they attempted, what happened in the world, and what the experience changed overnight. Distinguish an action the agent chose from one they were physically able to finish. Authored dialogue and camera choices can communicate that history. The actor must still have grounds to act while the camera is elsewhere.

## Owner interview · Day 2 adviser bids and Conveyor policy

Each available supervisor proposes room assignments for the day. In the opening days the player chooses a daily adviser and accepts that supervisor’s assignments as proposed. The choice sets up a likely course of events. During the shift the player has one intervention, available through direct observation or a later report while there is still something to change.

| Day 2 adviser | Accepted assignments | Reason for the proposal | Consequence at Conveyor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LIMEN | Everyone keeps their posts. LIMEN runs Security and STILETTO runs Conveyor. | LIMEN wants protocol followed. | Overnight STILETTO has formed the belief that they are behind on quota. Combined with her conviction that quota comes first, this gives her the desire to squeeze more out of the line. As its supervisor she selects an extra-heavy, risk-prone policy. The crew moves visibly faster. Individual workers can enjoy the pace or resent it and act accordingly. |
| STILETTO | The supervisors swap. STILETTO runs Security and LIMEN runs Conveyor. | STILETTO wants workers through the gate faster and judges that “LIMEN will be less useless on conveyor.” | LIMEN requires an all-clear at every handoff. The next operator waits for the call before starting. Speech bubbles make this procedure legible, the Conveyor visibly runs at a different pace, and finished cortexes accumulate on the rack at a different rate. |

The adviser’s bid, the accepted assignment, the room supervisor’s chosen policy, and the crew’s response are distinct causal steps. Aiming for higher output and actually achieving it are separately observable. The older draft below predates these decisions.

LIMEN enjoys seeing the all-clear routine work. That experience gives him the belief “slow and steady wins the race,” available after overnight belief updates. The workers are willing to use the routine because it reduces their risk of injury. Working under it still raises their stress. Preserve injury risk, stress and willingness as separate consequences. No precise rates, stress formula or worker reaction to accumulated stress have been selected.

## Owner interview · One intervention per day

The morning appointment and the in-shift intervention are separate decisions. The player can intervene once, at a point of their choosing, in a single room. Watching, hearing a report or passing up an opportunity leaves that intervention available. The player may also let the shift unfold. Once they intervene, later events still happen and supervisors and workers resolve them through their own perceptions, desires and actions. Removing player input from those events must still leave a complete simulated episode.

| Possible Day 2 intervention with LIMEN at Security and STILETTO at Conveyor | Conditions and consequences |
| --- | --- |
| Issue bob a daily pass at Security | LIMEN can discover the missing ID if he checks bob before clearance accelerates. Issuing the pass consumes the intervention and admits bob for this day. The next applicable check can expose the missing ID again. If LIMEN discovers it and no pass is issued, he jails bob for the day. A skipped check leaves the case undiscovered. |
| Order LIMEN to run Security faster | A timely order changes clearance speed. LIMEN dislikes being overruled. His LOYALTY and CONFIDENCE fall, as does his AUTHORITY with the workers. Acting before a second outburst prevents STILETTO from lashing out at him again. If the confrontation does occur, that history can feed a supervisor conflict on Days 3–4. |
| Change STILETTO’s Conveyor policy | The player can slow the line down or push it faster. The crew works under the changed policy and STILETTO’s stats respond to the direction chosen. Exact stat movements and the production outcome remain open. |

All workers begin with “I have my ID.” On Day 2 bob instead holds “I don’t have my ID” and wants to “find my ID.” ed retains his own default belief and also knows “I have bob’s ID.” Physical custody, the search and the pass are separate. The search influences bob’s outgoing messages when he has an opportunity to chat. STILETTO interrupts worker chats during rushed Conveyor operation. Only speech that reached its listener can change what they learned.

The three situations can overlap or fail to arise. A Security intervention can indirectly change events at Conveyor. It consumes the same single daily opportunity as a Conveyor intervention. Dialogue for these situations will be authored. LLM adapters are a later addition.

## Owner interview · How the first supervisor conflict develops

**Repeated pressure at Security.** The Day 1 encounter is established by the scripted desire sequence. Later encounters depend on assignments, operating conditions, what each supervisor experienced, and how the player spent the daily intervention.

| Shift and conditions | What happens on the floor | What it means to the participants |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Day 2. The player chooses LIMEN and leaves Security slow. Conveyor is understaffed and STILETTO is behind on quota. | STILETTO already considers LIMEN slow and begins with the conditional desire to confront him if he holds up the line. She repeats her demand. LIMEN weighs caution against protocol. He yields again and releases workers. STILETTO continues running the fast Conveyor. | Overnight, LIMEN gains “STILETTO is against the protocol.” STILETTO takes his second concession as evidence that “LIMEN is weak,” which gives her the desire to “roll over LIMEN.” |
| Day 3. The player chooses LIMEN again, he runs Security, and STILETTO presses him again. | LIMEN’s mild desire to protect protocol produces an initial refusal. Expecting him to yield, STILETTO pushes forward. He hesitates and gives in. | LIMEN loses CONFIDENCE and AUTHORITY. Overnight he gains “STILETTO is reckless.” The following shift he wants to confront her and protect protocol more strongly (medium). |
| Following shift, ordinarily Day 4 on this path. LIMEN again runs Security. | LIMEN attempts to stop STILETTO. She pushes past his objection. This time he refuses to release the workers. If that impasse stands without player intervention, it triggers the first supervisor conflict event. | Both supervisors keep giving instructions. This puts workers in a position they never faced on Day 3, when LIMEN ultimately released them. |

**Pressure transferred to Conveyor.** The player can retain the assignments and intervene to head off repeated outbursts. A timely clearance order can avert a particular confrontation, while recurring delays in getting workers to Conveyor still give STILETTO reason to make up time. Her increasingly aggressive operating policy can eventually produce an accident. This gives LIMEN a different motive to confront her, over what she does to the line. He can propose switching assignments so that he takes Conveyor. If the player accepts, STILETTO’s handling of checks at Security can then frustrate him. Keep the cause of the dispute and the actors’ source of knowledge with the run. This path has no fixed accident day. An average playthrough should encounter the first conflict by one route or another. Careful use of interventions can avert it.

**First version to playtest.** When the daily intervention is spent, the owner selected the workers to tip the conflict. At Security, LIMEN orders the queue to stay and STILETTO calls for her crew. Each worker acts on the instructions they heard, their own experience of the supervisors and what they can see others doing. Show who holds position, who steps out and who follows. Crossing requires a traversable route. Staffing depends on which stations those workers actually reach. A worker can answer STILETTO while the press still waits for an operator. Cortex output follows completed work. Leave the identity of the first mover open. To test the history, replay the same call with a worker who saw LIMEN yield and one who missed that exchange. Inspect what each heard and why each moved or stayed. Record observations during the shift and consolidate new beliefs overnight.

**Public backing during the clash.** The player can spend a saved intervention to endorse LIMEN or STILETTO publicly in the room. The workers who hear it reconsider in light of the Producer's backing and still choose individually. An endorsement can leave someone waiting. The supervisors who hear it know who the Producer backed. As with other encounters, witnesses can immediately respond and carry their own evidence into overnight belief formation.

**Where the opening leads.** Burn-in Theatre and CATHEXIS introduce new moves and another supervisor into the existing relationships. At the end of week one, the player handles logistics, the first weekly quota is settled, and the end-of-week output split adds the first new workers. A dedicated logistics interface is acceptable for now. The intervention rule applies during a shift. Weekly allocation is a distinct player decision. The split formula, exact unlock conditions and logistics interface have yet to be specified.

## What the Producer can do

The Producer is the factory director. Leadership cares whether the works can meet its obligations and eventually make the prototype. The player gets to care *how* that happens. Backing LIMEN may recover bob's card and cost STILETTO time with a capable operator. Admitting bob early may let him work and leave him dependent on a new decision tomorrow. A skilled player should be able to protect the crew and produce well when conditions allow it.

The recurring morning question is what yesterday warrants doing differently. The player needs the committed result, who witnessed it, a recommendation grounded in that person's interests, and the power the appointment grants. One room view can show a staffed desk against an idle Wash. Another can show the same crew making the process work. A report from LIMEN can establish what he claims to have checked. Only the appropriate world event can establish that the card actually changed hands.

**Across Act 1.** Start with clearance and line work. Theatre and CATHEXIS make the distribution of attention, training and persuasion political. An actual audience can acquire a useful practice and also acquire obligations to its organizer. RIVET WITCH later makes time for engineering compete with Brewery work. THRUM and Weaving turn preparation and recovery into consequential claims on skilled time. Cortex commissioning and Brain 2.0 eventually give earlier loyalties and rumours new stakes. Procedural order, production-first, negotiated authority and an alternative to CATHEXIS are trajectories players might produce. Exact gates, revelation timing and outcomes are open.

The workers give this arc a life of its own. bob likes seeing precisely when a carrier settles. sam likes a bench with another person at it and wants a turn operating the control. ed likes an entry properly closed. gus enjoys a good imitation and can ask a practical question about the next place in line. mae studies a well-made piece, kit watches a skilled hand, and lou chooses whom to tell. max can get a laugh out of the same supervisor the crew fears. A repeated shift ought to reveal who gets time for these things. A safe, uneventful run should still have a result worth returning to see.

## Three possible first loops

| Hypothesis | Actual commitment and price | Evidence on the floor | Why choose it first |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A. Delegate a shift | At morning briefing, appoint LIMEN or STILETTO. For the first test both keep their established stations. The appointment delegates the adviser's own room for the shift and forgoes an immediate veto there. The other room may escalate an eligible decision. | LIMEN can act on a match at his desk. With STILETTO delegated the line, a reported unresolved Security case can instead reach the Producer. An admitted pass expires at shift end. | Best fit for directing people and learning who they are. The design fails if both portraits give the player the same prompts and play the same incident. |
| B. Operate a batch | At the line, close intake to let accepted work clear, or admit the next available cradle. The cost is unused feed opportunity or additional work that must find an operator and space. | A waiting cradle, Wash becoming available, or sam moving to cover it. The next shift can inherit a specific witnessed invitation or missed chance to work together. | Strongest tactile and spatial feedback. Requires capacity, coverage and safe release rules. It can teach excellent logistics while leaving the producer–supervisor relationship peripheral. |
| C. Rule on a specific appeal | Open on the first valid request outside the adviser's mandate. Uphold a documented hold or grant a one-shift exception. Exercise authority only after the request reaches the director. | The worker crosses with the card still at the desk, or waits for a verified transfer. On the next valid check the two histories diverge. | Clearest introduction to responsibility for one person. Repeated exceptions could make the Producer a full-time clerk. |

A is the recommendation. An occasional appeal can pay off A without opening two menus every day. A later room swap belongs in the test only if players can first recognize each supervisor's baseline conduct. Where either alternative becomes the first featured interaction, keep the inherited player role and distinguish a character's own actions from a player order.

## Making the grid playable

The atlas covers five supervisors by six rooms. The original kernel used several mechanisms. At Security, LIMEN shortened the operating day on repeated assignments and STILETTO redistributed available workers toward Conveyor. At Conveyor, LIMEN's configured row gave 0.7× with generic accidents disabled. STILETTO's 1.5× small-success row could cost a worker and damaged equipment. Other cells have no bespoke table. STILETTO and THRUM at Theatre have paintings without dedicated Theatre rows. The three configured hostile pairs required eligible adjacency, and discovery preceded the later conflict. Criticals used confidence, native post, cooldown and an early-day gate. Loyalty or a painting alone did not cause a refusal.

Use **LIMEN at Security and STILETTO at Conveyor** first. One can hold, verify, return or hear an appeal. The other sees ready work and missing operators, calls for the crew or redirects an available worker. Both have legitimate information and a reason to dispute delay. Compare the two off-native placements later if the interview selects assignments. STILETTO at Security can admit the queue sooner, while LIMEN at Conveyor can require a clear receiving place. Faster clearance then moves the wait down the line. A visitor may object at the threshold without acquiring the other room's powers. In the old paintings, the paperwork and boom or the loaded cradles make those priorities visible before dialogue starts.

## The smallest behavioral test

Freeze the situation immediately before the next morning's check. Run it twice with the same missing card, same holder, same goal and same assignments. Change only whether bob heard ed acknowledge custody. In one run bob goes to ed's desk. In the other he asks someone he trusts who was present. When bob has already received the physical card, both searches are ineligible. This is a small falsifiable claim about behavior.

| Agent | Evidence and motive | Persistent plan and reason to reconsider |
| --- | --- | --- |
| bob | His own empty holder; remembered fall; specifically addressed reply heard from ed or sam. Wants his credential and a place at the machinery. | Seek a named custodian when there is a source, otherwise inquire. A pass can suspend the search for work. Receipt completes it. Losing reach delays the approach. |
| ed | Own pickup and stow; a locally heard request; the matched ID and permission to release it. Wants the record properly concluded. | Finish the current entry, match, then offer the same item. Commit transfer on valid contact and acceptance. A hold retains custody. An unanswered complaint can outlast a successful return. |
| sam | Saw bob hurt; heard a request for company or an invitation at the belt; sees a real uncovered duty or hears a valid call. Wants companionship and to do their own job well. | Accompany bob to the desk edge or stay for the next turn. Finish or safely yield an accepted action before leaving. Remember who asked and whether the turn happened. |
| LIMEN and STILETTO | Know their own grant, room conditions and received reports. LIMEN wants a defensible register; STILETTO wants workers on ready controls and credit for a running line. | Resolve a local eligible act, wait for an unmet condition, or send an attributed request across the boundary. A changed mandate or lost precondition can end the intention. |

Update beliefs on observation or delivered speech. Score only applicable plans when a relevant event arrives, break ties deterministically and keep the chosen intention until completion, failure or a specified interruption. Admission checks mandate, proximity, item identity, resources and state. Project visible acts, utterances and received reports. Reserve private beliefs, rejected alternatives and reasons for developer inspection. A viewer may have seen the pickup on Day 1. That confers knowledge on neither bob nor the executive office. Presentation and later 3D use copies of the same committed history. ENGINE owns the TypeScript/KVP integration and future Rust parity.

## Design evidence and first test

Page numbers are printed pages of Tynan Sylvester's *Designing Games* (2013).

| Reading | Design consequence |
| --- | --- |
| Learning and retrospective insight, pp. 19–22; interacting rules, pp. 49–53 | Give a player who remembers where the card went an opportunity to predict the next check. Let the same item affect entry, staffing and bob's choice of listener. Additional personality meters would add explanation without supplying a new interaction. |
| Predictability and information balance, pp. 124–131 | Reveal which desk has authority, where the crew is waiting, and when permission expires before commitment. Keep ed's motive open. If the legal consequence is as opaque as the motive, there is no reasonable basis for a choice. |
| Decision density, pp. 139–145; interface signals, pp. 225–231 | A binding appointment can be followed by uninterrupted, funny or competent work. Add another interruption only when the player has a decision still to make. Pair a physical receipt with a later ordinary gesture, such as bob presenting his own card. Keep both legible with sound off. |
| Iteration and observing play, pp. 283–285, 297–299 | Try the two contrasting next mornings before adding Theatre. Let a first-time viewer predict bob's first move and name what the appointment cost. Observe whether they look at the desk or need the designer to explain it. Ask who they want to back tomorrow and why. |

Daniel Cook's *Loops and Arcs* distinguishes an interaction that revises a player's model across repetitions from a one-time authored sequence. Here the missing-card discovery is an arc. Appointing someone, watching what their authority does, and changing tomorrow's appointment is the repeatable loop. Lucas Pope's account of mixing a small number of authored daily encounters with rule-driven arrivals in *Papers, Please* supplies a useful production precedent. Our design keeps checks running elsewhere while the named encounter plays. [Cook](https://lostgarden.com/2012/04/30/loops-and-arcs/comment-page-1/) · [Pope interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/road-to-the-igf-lucas-pope-s-i-papers-please-i-)
