# Epic: the studio becomes the engine

Editorial draft · 8 October 2026. Mirrored in the chapter content.

The technology used to build one game can become the product other studios build their games around.

Unreal arrived in 1998, the same year as Half-Life. Epic MegaGames, now Epic Games, developed it with Digital Extremes: a first-person action game set on an alien world. Making it also meant building software to draw that world, handle the player’s actions and provide tools for assembling its spaces. Epic’s Unreal Engine gave other developers a way to use that technology in games of their own. The audience for the game was the player; the customer for the engine was another creator.

The distinction matters because a successful game and useful production tools can earn in different ways. A player pays for an experience; a studio licensing an engine obtains technology on which years of work may depend. Epic must maintain the tools and support their use beyond its own games. Fortnite now makes that coexistence easy to see: Epic produces a game for players while supplying Unreal Engine to other teams. Its future can depend on what those teams make as well as on its next release.

## Four products, different agreements

Its store, launched in 2018, adds another role. A developer can sell a game there without asking Epic to make or publish it. Epic Games Publishing, announced in 2020, offers a different relationship: funding and release support for selected projects. The announcement promised developer ownership of the intellectual property and at least half the profit after costs were recovered. Putting a game in the store and accepting its owner as a publisher are separate decisions.

That difference creates different claims on a successful game. Engine licensing can produce a royalty; a store can retain part of a transaction; a publisher can recover investment and share the remaining proceeds. The contracts define when each applies. Epic’s standard store terms changed in 2025 to leave the first $1 million of annual net revenue per product with the developer, with an 88/12 split thereafter. That threshold is not the engine’s royalty threshold, even though both mention a million dollars.

## Whose revenue is it?

The distinction is visible in what companies report. Epic’s 2025 store review puts PC consumer spending at $1.16 billion, of which $400 million was on third-party games. The figures include taxes and exclude purchases processed by developers themselves inside their games. They do not tell us Epic’s revenue, let alone its profit. An analyst who multiplies the headline by one commission rate would mistake an audience’s spending for the platform’s income.

Buying a studio changes a different part of the arrangement. In 2019, Rocket League developer Psyonix announced it was joining Epic. That brings a developer into the group; publishing a partner’s project or licensing it an engine need not. A successful company can use its resources to expand in several directions. The record here establishes the acquisition, not which earlier hit paid for it.

## Infrastructure can become the product

Epic’s web-shop offering takes the idea further: it supplies selling infrastructure that another developer can use under its own game’s identity. The company has become a supplier to creative work as well as its producer. That makes the interests around a release harder to read from the logo alone. We have to identify the product, the customer and the agreement before we can ask what a change in player behavior is worth. Rockstar’s answer to the same problem takes us back toward the work itself: how a recognizable kind of world can sustain a franchise across releases, generations of hardware and years of play.
