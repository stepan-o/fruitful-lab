# Collective discovery of Mexico City

Status update: the later owner-approved full app scope and concrete prototype rules are specified in [EXPERIENCE_SPEC.md](EXPERIENCE_SPEC.md). This document retains the earlier discussion; its “later mode” boundaries are superseded where the new specification differs.

Roadmap idea recorded 10 October 2026. **The current priority remains the private multiplayer game for Susy and Stepan.** This complementary community experience is a later possibility to investigate, not an implementation commitment or a prerequisite for their game. Its appeal, participation and sustainability are untested.

## The invitation

A public map gradually gains color as people contribute discoveries from real outings. Someone can browse a quest without registering, do it outside and keep the experience to themselves. Another person can optionally submit a photo, place and short observation to a shared discovery logbook. Accepted contributions enrich the map and advance collective milestones that reveal further prompts.

The proposed emotional reward is: **“My small discovery helped us see another part of our city.”** Whether this motivates participation is the central question. People need not know each other, meet simultaneously or maintain accounts to contribute to the same result.

The later [goals discussion](GOALS_AND_CHALLENGES.md) places the community's overall objective at the shared-map level. System-curated quests can help reach it; there is no required individual daily checklist, account or competitive penalty. Opponent-issued timed challenges belong to the private multiplayer mode.

## Ways to participate

| Choice | Experience | Account requirement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Look around | Browse the map, logbook and available prompts. | None. |
| Take a quest outside | Do the activity and enjoy it privately; reporting is optional. | None. |
| Contribute | Submit a photo, specified place and optional caption; use an alias or publish without a name. | None in the proposed community version. |
| Keep a personal collection | Optionally attach contributions to a persistent profile. | A later option, using existing accounts if implemented. |
| Meet other explorers | Optionally attend an explicitly hosted public activity. | Separate later design; never a condition of contributing. |

The public logbook records contributions, not every outing. An unreported experience still fulfills the purpose of getting someone to explore. Do not count anonymous submissions as unique people, or interpret silence as inactivity. Browsing, contributing and public identification are separate choices. No account requirement does not mean a promise of technical untraceability.

## How the map could change

Start with a readable pale sketch, retaining neighborhood names and useful orientation. A first accepted discovery adds a colorful detail. A mix of distinct places or perspectives gradually adds texture. A clearly described milestone reveals a new themed set of prompts. Color also needs a textual or patterned equivalent for accessibility.

For example, a neighborhood prompt might invite people to notice overlooked facade details. Several contributions from different blocks could reveal a collective photo chapter and a new observation quest. The exact count is a trial parameter, not a settled rule. No single contribution should finish an entire borough.

Gray means **“not yet represented in this community logbook”**, not empty, uninteresting or unsafe. A neighborhood never becomes completely known. New perspectives, seasons and stories can add to it after an initial milestone. Areas with fewer submissions can receive interesting prompts without treating distance, isolation or risk as a higher-value achievement.

Keep starter activities available across areas so a quiet neighborhood is not blocked by a lack of players. Reserve collective unlocks for additional content or themes. Favor distinct contributions and varied perspectives over raw upload volume; repeating a photo or flooding one location should not advance the same milestone repeatedly. The map reflects submissions accepted for publication, not a verified measure of physical visits or local culture.

For a first community trial, test shared progress without individual competitive rankings or prizes. This is a proposal to isolate the appeal of the collective result; points can remain part of the separate private battle game.

## Participation and safety choices

The owner explicitly raised safe participation by strangers and people of different ages. The proposed initial community experience is asynchronous. Publish discoveries at public places or an appropriately broad area, never live player locations or movement trails. Review submissions before they appear, with a clear report/removal route. Supply an unguessable private management link for a contribution if there is no account; do not force signup just to request removal.

Submission should clearly show which photo, location and caption will become public. Remove photo location metadata, avoid publishing contact information, and allow a relevant object or scene instead of a person's portrait. Keep private battle records separate; do not copy them into the community logbook without a deliberate publication choice.

Offer short, free and accessible prompts alongside longer outings. Do not require purchases, night exploration or interaction with strangers. General public participation should not automatically enable direct messages, live proximity or contact between adults and minors. If hosted walks or all-ages gatherings become part of the idea, design the facilitation and age-appropriate arrangements separately. Registration alone would not establish that a person is safe.

## What a later small trial should learn

Begin with a bounded area and a handful of prepared quests once the private game is enjoyable. Invite a small voluntary group, explain that this is a prototype and collect feedback openly. An initial exercise could run for two weekends with around ten to twenty invited participants; those are practical proposal numbers, not a sample size that establishes general demand.

Observe which prompts people choose, whether they report trying something new, whether they voluntarily contribute, whether they return to see others' discoveries, and how much work publication review creates. Offer an optional way to tell us they enjoyed an outing without sharing the photo publicly. Ask what made the experience worthwhile and what discouraged participation.

Keep three questions separate:

1. Is the outing itself enjoyable?
2. Does adding something to a shared map feel meaningful?
3. Does seeing others' contributions encourage another outing or an optional social connection?

A small uncontrolled trial can reveal friction and generate hypotheses. It cannot establish that collective progress caused more exploration, prove demand across ages, or measure all unreported outings. If that causal question later matters, compare clearly defined versions with willing participants and a suitable evaluation design. For now, useful research terms are participatory design, collective action, locative games and community participation.

## Reading and precedents

Sources checked 10 October 2026. These inform questions and design choices; none establishes that this proposed game will work.

1. **Nina Simon, The Participatory Museum.** Start with [Chapter 3, From Me to We](https://participatorymuseum.org/chapter3/), on connecting individual contributions into a shared experience without requiring everyone to interact directly. Follow with [Chapter 10, Evaluating Participatory Projects](https://participatorymuseum.org/chapter10/) to think through what participation is intended to achieve. The [complete book is free to read online](https://participatorymuseum.org/read/). Our design question: can one small contribution visibly improve something for everyone?
2. **Mapatón CDMX.** This [case study from Living Cities and NDI, republished by the German Marshall Fund](https://www.gmfus.org/download/article/21466), describes the 2016 collective transit-mapping game, including incentives for unmapped routes and subsequent data review. It is a close local precedent for contributing to a shared city map. It also used prizes and organized recruitment, so it does not demonstrate that an anonymous, reward-free version would attract or retain people. The original LabCDMX page was unavailable during this check.
3. **Blast Theory, Rider Spoke.** The [artists' project account](https://www.blasttheory.co.uk/projects/rider-spoke/) describes cycling through a city, recording personal stories at chosen places and listening to strangers' recordings there. It offers a model of connection through places and asynchronous contributions. Its event format is different from an open public website, and we need not adopt its nighttime cycling setting.
4. **Robert E. Kraut and Paul Resnick, Building Successful Online Communities.** The [MIT Press description](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262528917/building-successful-online-communities/) explains its use of social-science evidence for contribution and commitment. Read when considering why people would return and how a community can be maintained beyond its launch. This is a research-informed design reference, not a requirement to build a conventional social network.

These sources suggest a useful framing: a participatory city game, explored through an openly described social prototype. Keep that inquiry alongside, and later than, the immediate goal of making the two-person game fun.
