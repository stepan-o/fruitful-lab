Not One Marigold

The article needs an early identity box: cempasúchil / *Tagetes erecta* is not the same as pot marigold / calendula. Many grief, food, and Shakespeare claims belong to calendula, while Día de Muertos belongs to cempasúchil.

Kew TagetesSBT

Color, Scent, Return

INAH and ASyD support cempasúchil as a sensory flower of return: petals mark paths, aroma guides arrival, and altars/graveyards create a welcome for the dead.

INAHASyD PDF

The Timing Matters

The strongest academic twist is that cempasúchil meaning is cultivated. ASyD shows the modern late-October flowering/use as a syncretic adaptation of planting, harvest, calendar, and ritual practice.

ASyD PDF

Solar Warmth Detail

CONABIO/EncicloVida records a Malinalco-linked belief around Tonalxochitl: yellow flowers could hold the warmth of solar rays. This is a gold nugget for the Bloom Reveal.

CONABIO PDF

Grief Is Real, But Separate

Older flower-language and Shakespeare material supports marigold as grief, distress, tombs, deathbeds, and sun-following. The caveat: this layer is mainly calendula/pot marigold, not Mexican cempasúchil.

DumontSBT

Quick Answer / TLDR Box

Marigold can mean several flowers, especially Mexican cempasúchil (*Tagetes erecta*) and pot marigold/calendula (*Calendula officinalis*). Cempasúchil is tied to Día de Muertos, where color and aroma mark the path for loved ones returning to the altar. Older European marigold language can mean grief, while South Asian garlands often signal auspiciousness, devotion, and celebration.
