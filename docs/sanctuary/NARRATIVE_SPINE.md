# Sanctuary Economics — recovered argument and editorial discipline

4 October 2026. Recovered from the whole conversation by three dedicated readers:
thread synthesis, critical editing and primary-source verification. This is the
current editorial authority. Earlier implementation maps remain historical records.

## What the essay is investigating

The relationship between what people love about a game and what its creators need
to sell. A commercial arrangement can fund a pleasure, shape it, preserve it or
undermine it. Begin with actual decisions and their consequences; do not turn this
into a repeated people/work/payment formula or a catalogue of villains.

The owner's arcade conversation changed the scale of the question. The player
buys a turn; an operator collects coins; a venue may gain a livelier evening.
Those are related, but the receipt does not exhaust the experience's value.
Gauntlet then supplies concrete surprises: simultaneous cooperative play had an
earnings argument, monster generation could change the paid opportunity without
changing the quarter price, and a final ending could invalidate purchased health.
The economic arrangement was already inside the creative form.

The opening's argument is carried by these discoveries. Its moral complexity
should be visible in the facts, without repeatedly explaining that nobody is evil.

## Insights that must survive future edits

- Selling imaginary benefits is old. Calling them “air” reveals their constructed
  nature; it does not establish that the experience is worthless. A cinema ticket
  also purchases participation in something that ends.
- Several businesses can see different value in the same activity. Do not make
  every venue owner a miniature revenue-optimization department, or assume every
  adjustment benefits every participant.
- Revenue and pleasure can be produced by the same design choice. Logg's
  four-player story is evidence; exclusive financial origin would be an overclaim.
  His creative inspirations also included Dungeons & Dragons and Dandy.
- A purchased copy separates another attempt from another payment. Diablo II's
  classes, builds, loot and repeated runs already made it deeply replayable.
  StarCraft's longevity is another useful counterexample to replay = recurring sales.
- “Once you bought it, you got it all” concerns the systems and possibilities in
  the purchased release. An expansion is another bounded work, not evidence that
  the original purchase included all future work. Avoid imprecise ownership claims.
- Diablo's combat, experience, equipment and character development remain
  recognizable while the surrounding product changes. Do not invent replayability,
  progression or player attachment as features that arrived with live services.
- The shape of a playthrough is distinct from the price model. Diablo IV contains
  a campaign and seasonal runs; Diablo II already had replay and later ladder
  seasons. Introduce those systems when they become relevant, with dates and context.
- A maintained service does not entail a separate recurring fee. Diablo II and
  free Battle.net must prevent a false services-versus-owned-works binary.
- Studios need cash between major releases. Continuing sales of a released work,
  expansions, subscriptions and other offers finance different obligations. Do not
  invent internal motives or imply every existing player must fund the gap alone.
- BG3 and Diablo IV can share lengthy play, role-playing, builds and replay while
  being built and sold under different arrangements. This is a contemporary fork,
  not a division into story lovers and system lovers, or short and long games.
- Books/rereading and continuing series clarify purchase expectations; cinema,
  Netflix and Adobe clarify creative production and funding. Each comparison
  must explain one relationship, then return to games. D4 is not a Netflix subscription.
- Audience affection is part of the economic story. A game may become a place
  people meet; a service cannot manufacture its audience merely by delivering
  content. Concord deserves its own treatment of that uncertainty and anxiety.

## The long arc, and the state of the manuscript

The intended progression remains arcade origins → the long history of purchases
and continuing play → the contemporary fork and Concord → Diablo from its original
innovation onward → design, engagement and monetization layers → focused cases.
Introduce products, studios and systems for unfamiliar readers without a generic
“what gaming is” lesson. A veteran reader supplies recognition, not required context.

## Write first, compose second

1. Recover the relevant argument from this document and the whole conversation,
   not only the latest correction. Review the neighbouring chapters' jobs.
2. Write a complete chapter outside the component layout. Every paragraph must
   change what the reader understands; an abstract summary is not a new discovery.
3. Read the draft continuously. Challenge its causal claims, transitions,
   redundancies and assumptions about the reader. Verify historical particulars
   against original sources. Keep methodological qualifications in evidence notes
   unless the argument itself depends on them.
4. Edit the whole draft before placing it on the page. Do not patch individual
   blocks in response to a conceptual correction while retaining a broken arc.
5. Assign each visual a different job: establish a setting, reveal a mechanism,
   compare conditions or show evidence. Remove prose that merely feeds empty UI.
   A caption, diagram and takeaway must not repeat the same paragraph four times.
6. Preserve the visual-first opening and performance/accessibility rules. A good
   manuscript is not a reason to place a wall of text before the first image.
7. Verify the rendered reading sequence as well as the code. Tests can protect
   placement and controls; they cannot establish that an essay is good.
