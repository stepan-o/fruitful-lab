# The work of making a world

Before a world can earn its keep, someone has to decide what we can do there—and how it will answer.

When Miles Tost joined work on The Witcher 3, one of his first assignments was to place ideas on an internal map. The game, made by Polish studio CD PROJEKT RED, follows Geralt, a professional monster hunter, through a world of wars, local troubles and difficult obligations. Before the player could encounter any of that, somebody had to decide what belonged where. Tost remembers being surprised by how many of those early markers survived into the finished game.

The map was a beginning. A place needed inhabitants, a reason to visit and something that could happen there. Writers, artists, designers, performers and programmers had to make those pieces work together. A conversation might require a room, a face, a voice, a camera position and rules for when each line could be spoken. Translation and testing would have to follow every meaningful variation. The player sees an encounter; the studio has produced a small collaboration inside a much larger one.

Discussing that work in 2026, Tost and fellow designer Marta Dobińska describe a recurring choice: which possibilities deserve a response? They want players to have freedom, but a new consequence can require more dialogue, different spaces or additional rules. Tost asks whether the player will remember it. An elaborate response that nobody notices is a poor return on the care spent making it.

## A place has more than one way to come alive

Rockstar approaches the same problem through the density of life around the player. Red Dead Redemption 2 follows Arthur Morgan, an outlaw whose gang is losing its place in a changing America. Its written story sits inside a world of travel, hunting, camp life and encounters between missions. In a 2018 interview, Rockstar’s Rob Nelson explained that caring for a horse would improve its response to the player. Equipment carried on the animal gave the relationship a practical importance. Familiarity with a place could grow through the things a player repeatedly did there.

These games overlap far more than a simple contrast between story and simulation suggests. Both use written scenes, performances and interacting systems. What differs is the emphasis of a particular moment. An authored exchange can give a character exactly the right answer. A reusable behavior can let many characters react to circumstances the designer did not stage one by one. The first demands attention to each scene; the second demands rules that continue to make sense when they meet other rules. A convincing world usually needs both.

Nintendo’s The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom makes another investment visible. Its hero can join objects into contraptions and use them to cross a fantasy landscape. The developers describe building the game around the expectation that players will try their own solutions. A useful object belongs to many possible situations. Creating that freedom involves making interactions reliable, arranging places where they matter and testing combinations. The player’s invention becomes part of the spectacle the studio is selling.

RimWorld, made by Ludeon Studios, brings the people inside the simulation closer to the foreground. The player manages a colony whose inhabitants have needs, injuries, relationships and work to do. An event director introduces pressures such as raids and storms. The resulting history belongs to that particular colony. Bay 12’s Dwarf Fortress carries a related idea through generated civilizations, individual lives and a world that records changes across successive games. Their visual simplicity can conceal a great deal of work: the drama depends on many small conditions meeting at the wrong—or unexpectedly right—time.

These are different ways of spending a production budget on a world’s responsiveness. They also create different things worth returning for: another outcome in a written story, greater familiarity with a place, an experiment with its rules, or a history nobody else has lived. None requires charging for each return. A simulation can support years of play from one purchase. A beautifully authored expansion can justify another sale. The creative structure and the commercial offer influence one another without being the same thing.

## The machinery beneath the place

A game engine supplies much of the common machinery: drawing the world, moving objects, playing sound and letting a team assemble and test what it makes. Around it sit production tools for animation, writing, recording, collaboration and release. Some studios develop much of this technology themselves. Others license a general engine and build their distinctive systems on top. In either case, the budget has to support both the experience and the means of producing it.

CD PROJEKT RED’s move from its own REDengine to Epic’s Unreal Engine makes that decision unusually visible. Announced in 2022 for a new Witcher saga, the agreement initially ran for fifteen years, covered an unlimited number of games and included dedicated technical support. It did not turn the existing Cyberpunk 2077 into an Unreal game. The studio was changing how future work would be made, while continuing to support work built with its earlier technology.

The partnership also involved developing the tools. In 2025, the two companies showed a Witcher 4 technology demonstration, running on a PlayStation 5, with systems for dense environments and crowds. It was a demonstration of technology, not a promise that the finished game would behave identically. Its significance is the collaboration: a studio’s needs can become improvements to an engine that other teams also use. Epic supplies infrastructure; CD PROJEKT RED still has to decide what its world is for and do the work of making it particular.

That infrastructure has business models of its own. Unreal’s standard game licence can collect royalties after a revenue threshold; Unity combines a free tier with paid plans for qualifying teams; Godot distributes its engine under the MIT licence. Those choices place different obligations around production. An engine without a royalty still needs people who can use, adapt and maintain it. A licensed engine can save a team from building shared machinery while creating dependence on another company’s priorities and terms. The meaningful comparison is the work a team can sustain, not the price of a download.

## Loopforge: give the consequences a voice

Loopforge is my own experiment in this territory: a factory game and a simulation-and-narrative engine being developed around it. The factory makes artificial minds. Five supervisors bring expertise, temperament and conflicting interests to the production floor. You need their help to meet the quota. In the game’s developing story, having authority over them does not settle what they will do with your orders.

That is a smaller stage than Night City or the American frontier, but it asks a demanding question. Can a decision about production become an event in a relationship? A supervisor who welcomes more output and one who distrusts the pressure behind it have different reasons to judge the same shift. Authored personalities give the disagreement its shape. The simulation has to supply something concrete for them to disagree about.

Loopforge separates those responsibilities. The simulation resolves a decision and records what changed. A narrative process receives that evidence and the selected character’s voice. It can express approval, suspicion or pride; its words cannot alter the recorded outcome. The working principle is “Truth stays clean. Story gets messy.” A reliable world can contain an unreliable witness, provided the system keeps the witness’s account separate from what actually happened.

The current web prototype makes a small part of that architecture inspectable. Over eight shifts, assignments and a production policy affect output and strain. These are the prototype’s rules, not evidence about real workplaces. The broader game’s planned loyalties, discoveries and refusals remain work to build.

The opportunity is to make more of those consequences perceptible without writing every possible response in advance. It still takes writing to establish a character, engineering to constrain the evidence and judgment to decide whether a line belongs in the scene. A language model can produce fluent words that misstate an event. Separating narration from the simulation protects the mechanics; it does not by itself make every sentence true or every reaction worth reading.

That gives analytics a useful job. Can a player explain what their decision changed? Can they distinguish the supervisors’ priorities? Do they remember the exchange later? Those observations belong beside response time, contradictions and the cost of producing an acceptable line. Generating more text is easy to count. Producing a moment that makes someone care about the next decision is the result worth investigating.

## A world still needs an audience

The economic promise of better tools is that a team can spend more of its effort on experiences people value. Sometimes that means an encounter staged with exceptional care. Sometimes it means rules that let an unplanned story unfold. Loopforge is one attempt to connect those approaches, and its prototype gives us a way to examine the attempt before mistaking the ambition for an achievement.

But the ability to make a world does not establish a business for it. The people who might love it have other games, other commitments and only so many evenings. A production plan can be technically credible and still depend on an audience that never gathers. Concord brings that uncertainty into view: the costs of building and launching a new world arrived before the continuing business around it had proved itself.
