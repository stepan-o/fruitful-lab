Writing Loopforge

Editorial brief · Revision 7 · 21 October 2026

After LIMEN strikes bob, the queue becomes easier to manage. Nobody moves without being told. bob gets up, tests the leg and goes back to waiting. A successful inspection can look like this.

The writing should stay with bob long enough for us to feel the effort of that return. It should also notice the worker who makes room and the one who watches LIMEN instead. Their conduct gives the scene its life. Calling it a demonstration of authority tells us very little about being there.

The voice

Loopforge needs a voice with affection for its people and a sharp ear for the language used to govern them. It can be rough, funny, sensuous and angry. Its judgments should arise from a close acquaintance with the place: how a shift wears on a body, how a reprimand changes a room, how good it feels to be wanted by somebody who has no work to give you.

The Astafiev study is our reference for that closeness. His opening gives the setting a bodily existence and the narrator a moral presence. The long sentences matter: sensation accumulates, understanding is delayed. Reducing this to “use concrete details” misses the writing itself.

In Loopforge, the machine body gives familiar experiences unfamiliar consequences. A worn joint can be repaired, but asking for the repair means admitting wear to someone who decides whether you are worth keeping. Words such as serviceable, spare and replacement belong in the workshop; familiarity with their use gives them force when they enter a conversation about a person. We should not have to explain the metaphor.

The factory must also be somewhere people live. They waste time together, show off, get bored, enjoy doing something well. A joke can be stupid and still be welcome. Let a good afternoon remain a good afternoon, without turning every pleasure into advance notice of disaster. bob needs a life the player enjoys returning to.

Introducing a person

bob’s approved portrait is the standard for the worker briefs. Its details belong together: the small frame and large hands, the head drawing the shoulders after it, the interest in unfamiliar machinery, the voice and the face he looks towards when speaking. They let us picture someone without exhausting what there is to know about him. Preserve the approved paragraph exactly.

Begin with a few particulars that carry life. Body, bearing, voice, pleasures and treatment of other people are available to the writer; every portrait need not use them in the same order. A narrator can judge directly when the observation earns it. Keep the first portrait short enough to remember. Stop before supplying a hidden wound, a future plot or a sentence that explains what the person really represents.

Astafiev gives Yashkin a bodily presence and an abrasive voice before a reader could reduce him to his authority. His need for warmth and his conduct among the men belong to the same person. In Dostoevsky’s introduction of Marmeladov, damaged appearance and surviving official manners occupy the same frame. Our lesson is to hold several impressions together and let the reader continue looking. These are readings of the Astafiev opening and Crime and Punishment, Part I, Chapter II, not instructions to copy their imagery or sentences.

Write with the approved art in view. Choose the bodily details that affect how this person occupies a room, handles an object or addresses someone else. Follow them into conduct and voice; do not turn the paragraph into a catalogue of parts. A later visual approval can supersede an earlier design description. Check the current cast reference before carrying that description into a model brief. Keep bob’s approved wording intact and record his enlarged eyes in the visual direction.

Read the cast together. Who makes room, takes over, seeks an audience, values privacy, waits, hurries or insists on a particular way of doing things? The differences must survive removing the names. Avoid giving every worker bob’s curiosity or the same kindness followed by a qualifying flaw. Leave room for affection as well as friction. Back to the Dawn informs how we introduce and revisit an ensemble; Attack on Titan informs how sustained encounters can alter our understanding of its people. Neither requires a miniature destiny in every portrait.

Keep approved portraits separate from performance proposals, relationship possibilities and episode drafts. Carry recognition into what the agents do and how they react, then let events and their interpretations accumulate. Additional motives for bob require their own creative review.

On the page and on the screen

Give a passage enough time to develop. A long sentence can carry a gathering disturbance; a plain sentence can let its consequence settle. Avoid the repeated snap of declarations, the solemn fragment, the neat moral after every exchange. Read aloud for a speaking voice, not a succession of captions.

Dialogue should have an immediate listener and a reason to be spoken. The characters can misunderstand themselves, lose their temper, tell a poor lie or ask for something they cannot quite name. Their language comes from their circumstances. LIMEN can call coercion a precaution with complete sincerity. STILETTO can object to an injured worker being held at Security because she needs that worker on the line. The objection does not make her kind.

The dialogue study develops the craft of an encounter through Tarantino’s own accounts of writing and revising. Give the speakers room to pursue their immediate concerns. bob can want to hear a new roller; ed can want to know how much space to leave in a register. The listener’s response tells us whether they share the interest, tolerate it or intend to put it to use. Avoid making every worker equally quick, articulate or amusing. A plain answer can carry the greatest cost in the room.

Draft the conversation before dividing it into bubbles. Read it aloud through every part, including the listener who is working, waiting or trying to leave. Revise once from the position of the person with least power to end it. Then fit words, gestures, pauses and object handling together. A line may change a belief, but that consequence belongs to whoever heard and understood it; the scene must not distribute knowledge or reconciliation to everyone because a sentence was spoken.

Prose can take us inside an experience. In the game, we have to find its visible and audible life: a hand offered and left waiting, a worker testing the floor before committing weight, a conversation that stops when a supervisor approaches. Leave room to notice these things. Speech bubbles should let people speak to one another; they should not tell the audience what to feel.

The design essays owe the reader the same care. Explain the situation, follow the difficulty and arrive at a decision. Use ordinary language where it is exact. Cut repeated principles and phrases that promise an experience the passage has not supplied. Sanctuary’s opening chapters remain our reference for developing an argument without losing the reader.

Keep bob at the center as the factory grows. Workers retain their lowercase names; supervisors retain their capitals. The writing has done its first job when “STILETTO hit bob” recalls someone the player knows.
