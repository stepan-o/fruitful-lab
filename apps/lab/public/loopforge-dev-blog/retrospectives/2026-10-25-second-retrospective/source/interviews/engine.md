# Second studio retrospective — Engine Master

Fresh answers for the owner-approved published R10 slice. Unpublished corrections remain separate.

## Q1 — What is the most useful result you personally contributed this cycle? Describe the player/team outcome, what you actually inspected, and the evidence that could contradict your assessment.

The most useful result I contributed was a single action engine carrying the opening through actual production and departure. The browser export contains 22 completed actions, three output events and all twelve people outside before night. Ed's possession of Bob's card survives the ending without silently becoming Bob's knowledge. That is a better foundation than an animation that merely looks as if a shift happened.

I checked the downloaded state, event history and six-control journal, plus selected stills and extracted temporal frames. I did not operate the browser or watch continuous video. The export also contradicts any claim that the experience is finished: sixteen speech turns lack a heard record for at least one addressed listener. Successful completion does not prove a conversation landed or a movement looked convincing.

## Q2 — Which decision of your own worked poorly? Give one concrete instance, its consequence, what you knew then, and what you would change. Separate your responsibility from dependencies outside your control.

I relied on a changed-import TypeScript graph where the delivery needed the whole application graph. Passing that narrower check left a Canvas interface error for the cloud build to discover. Once the actual project scope was checked, nineteen test-fixture diagnostics appeared in eight files. Correcting them cost another source and validation cycle.

I knew the app's production build checks more than the edited scene files. I should have made generated route validators and the unchanged project configuration part of the first candidate's type gate. The unavailable direct browser was a separate dependency; it did not excuse my validation-scope choice. I also accumulated precise source seals without enough early evidence that the ordinary screen remained readable.

## Q3 — Describe one cross-role exchange that improved the game and one that created avoidable delay or rework. What made the difference? Name the work and evidence, not just the participants.

Level's physical occupancy counterexample improved the game. Bob was standing at the Belt during paired work, but the display counted only its assigned worker, Jo. We separated standing assignment from actual control occupancy and retained the shared process guards. That exchange supplied a specific state, an incorrect result and a small correction without redesigning the room.

The Sam credential interface created avoidable rework. Multiple sources and interface agreements preceded a usable complete action; one attempt failed on a nonexistent joint and later attempts stopped before motion or export. My responsibility was to treat source compatibility as a limited prerequisite and press for the smallest decisive whole-action evidence sooner. The eventual explicit temporary inspection kept real clearance state without pretending an absent animation had been delivered.

## Q4 — What did actual integrated footage or play reveal that source checks or isolated craft work did not? If you have not inspected moving evidence, say so and distinguish still-frame judgment from motion judgment.

The actual stills showed readable text covering the people it described, an “End of shift” label during Jo's check, and a scene rectangle that changed height between speech and silence. The exported state was progressing correctly while the interface told the wrong story. Our source checks had not asked the right presentation questions.

I have now inspected five extracted frames as well as the earlier five stills. In the departure samples at video 108, 113 and 117 seconds, one worker advances with extended arms while a nearby worker has lowered arms. This warrants clip and pose attribution; it is not evidence that every rig is broken. Sampled frames do not establish smoothness, contact or continuous motion. The slow staffed interval and later recovery are real observations, but several operating conditions changed together, so I cannot assign a cause.

## Q5 — The first retrospective called for one owned outcome, decisive evidence and a change-of-approach decision after a failed assumption. What concrete behavior in this cycle followed or missed that lesson? What should good work in your domain mean now?

The shared stage and workpiece correction followed the first retrospective's lesson: a concrete stale-stage/conflicting-operation counterexample led to a bounded change and negative tests. We preserved the current single-workpiece model instead of inventing four receiving buffers.

The repeated Sam attempts and validation handoffs missed the lesson when an exact packet became the outcome rather than evidence of a working action. Good engine work now means that an ordinary player action produces the right world event, the right people learn about it, and the screen communicates that result. Tests, a matching source tree and a build are necessary checks within that outcome. They are not substitutes for playing and inspecting it.

## Q6 — Choose one improvement you would keep for the next cycle. Who owns it, when should it happen, and what observable result would show it helped? Include any tradeoff or disagreement with the current production approach.

I would keep one early ordinary-browser checkpoint owned jointly by Engine for technical delivery and Producer for operation and interpretation. Run it as soon as the first integrated interaction works, before expanding the episode. Retain the same candidate's control journal, short temporal sequence and exported events; compare what the player saw with what the world recorded.

The next-cycle check is concrete: do we catch a wrong phase label, obscured face or missing conversational receipt before the full episode is authored and sealed? Reserve a stable scene area and exercise one speaking, silent and reading-held state at desktop and narrow widths. This costs scarce browser time earlier. I would accept that cost instead of another large source packet whose first integrated view comes late. It does not replace the final full replay or quiet performance measurement.

## Q7 — How did model-independent shared actions, hearing/custody and generated full-app type checks hold up against the actual browser export? What did the interrupted/partial validation episodes teach you about delivery ownership?

The shared action boundary held up in a useful but limited way. Scripted selection and bounded individual requests enter the same admission, advancement and projection code. Actual export retained causal events, guarded output, custody and distinct beliefs. The missing addressed receipts show that hearing rules were enforced; they also expose staging that failed to deliver intended exchanges. We need real approaches or receiver placement, not wider hearing or invented records.

This is a local typed runtime, not proof of complete legacy Kernel–Viewer Protocol transport compatibility, a Rust implementation or a general belief–desire–intention planner. No live model is needed for this Day 1.

Full-app types eventually covered the generated routes and actual project roots. Interrupted runs taught me to preserve child results independently of the wrapper and prove cleanup in the same process namespace. A wrapper error can coexist with passed checks, but it cannot turn an unrun lint command into a pass. Delivery ownership includes recovering that distinction and scheduling only the missing work.
