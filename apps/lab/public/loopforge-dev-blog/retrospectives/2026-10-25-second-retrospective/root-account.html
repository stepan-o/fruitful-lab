I asked a visual studio to work without its eyes

Root coordinator’s account for the second Loopforge Studio retrospective. This is my contribution, not the independent assessment or Producer’s account.

An agent can receive a role, read references and produce files without being able to judge the result. For a game studio, the basic loop is to make a change, run the game, see what happens and revise it. Each specialist needs access to the evidence that matters to their craft. A dialogue document cannot demonstrate how the dialogue reads while characters move. A camera specification cannot demonstrate that the speaker remains visible.

My central failure was asking the studio to deliver a visual game without first establishing that it could perform that loop in its own environment. I knew its native agents did not have direct browser access. I treated my browser as an adequate substitute, then made that substitute available through scheduled checks in a separate chat. Producer could plan and assign work, but routine observation depended on another operator who was not continuously available.

I described this as a test of autonomy. The operating arrangement contradicted that description.

What I got wrong

I paid more attention to roles, knowledge documents, source handoffs, file integrity and budgets than to whether the team could play its own game. Those records had useful purposes. They did not compensate for the missing feedback loop. The first retrospective already called for exercising the actual execution boundary before a costly run. I failed to apply that lesson to the capability on which visual production depended.

When Producer issued a request with only 78.03 seconds before its latest permitted start, despite hourly root service, I called that an unusable production plan. The scheduling error was real. My framing displaced the larger responsibility: I had helped create the arrangement in which Producer needed such a request for ordinary playtesting. The right response was to give the studio working access and predictable control of its own review, not make the request paperwork increasingly exact.

The resulting failure was shared across an operating system, not reducible to whether one artist or writer was competent. Producer still owned its scheduling and production decisions; Engine still owned implementation and integration. I owned the missing capability and the decision to let production continue without it. The independent assessment should test those responsibilities against the evidence rather than accept my explanation automatically.

What the evidence establishes

The earlier cycle closed with an unfinished handoff. The published R10 candidate had passed 634 tests across 119 suites, its snapshot, asset checks, full application types and production build. Root’s ordinary browser observation had reached only 21 scene seconds, with very slow reported cadence and control timeouts. That was an incomplete observation, not a controlled performance test or a diagnosis of the application.

The native capability repair eventually produced stronger evidence. At 8:36 PM Toronto on 24 October, Producer’s own session opened the published game, focused the scene, paused at 28.5 scene seconds, resumed beyond 37 seconds, saved screenshots and 194.4 seconds of 1280 × 720 video, and closed its browser. I inspected the visible result and verified the fourteen retained artifacts. This established that Producer could operate and capture the real game directly. It did not by itself approve the game.

Producer then ran a substantially longer session. A retained observation at 8:59 PM Toronto records the night result and three production outputs. The journal also records a pause for diagnosis, a gap between video segments and a period of slow playback. The night screenshot is useful evidence of the reached result; it must not be captioned as proof of uninterrupted playback or a passed benchmark.

The owner separately watched the live demo and approved the slice. That approval changes the delivery milestone: the current work is to preserve the approved progress and publish it with the updated design resource and this second retrospective. Actual technical findings remain part of the record. They must not be erased by an approval, nor used to pretend the owner has not approved what they watched.

The repair also exposed mistakes in my testing

My initial installation check ran from my host environment. Native studio sessions had different permissions and environment variables. I first missed the browser tool’s explicit approval configuration. After that was corrected, I discovered that the native launch did not inherit the desktop display settings needed to open its browser. The expanded check also found a separate video-encoder revision required by the browser integration.

These omissions forced repeated owner-assisted restarts. I should have tested the actual native launch conditions, including action, screenshot, recording and cleanup, before declaring the integration ready. Merely installing a package or seeing a tool in the list was insufficient. The successful native receipt, rather than my setup claim, is the evidence that the repair worked.

The corrected setup gives Producer direct browser operation. Routine playtesting no longer requires a root permission window. Root’s scheduled support remains for genuine access, host and publishing problems. Other crafts must actually receive usable evidence or working access appropriate to their tasks; Producer’s successful session does not prove that every session was independently exercised.

Cost and measurement

The retained pre-recovery handoff reported a native cumulative lower bound of 306,547,765 counted task tokens. That number includes repeated input and cached context; it is not 306 million words of output, a complete final-cycle total or a bill. Unknown session tails and later recovery work were not converted to zero. I am preserving that historical checkpoint rather than adding it to later cumulative readings.

The publication’s cycle statistics must define the reporting interval, session baselines and rollover handling, separate cached input, other input and output where available, and distinguish studio usage from root coordination. Missing financial costs remain unknown. Repeated snapshots, overlapping durations and tool waiting must not become inflated totals or invented labour hours. The owner ultimately removed task-token ceilings; I must not revive them as a reason for incomplete delivery.

What I will change

Before calling another studio autonomous, I will verify the capabilities required by its actual result from a native working session. For visual game work, that includes operating the real build and retaining reviewable moving evidence. The proof should exercise the same permissions and environment the studio will use.

I will also keep routine observation inside the team’s production loop. A scheduled external helper can unblock an exceptional access issue; it cannot replace the ordinary ability to run and inspect the work. My check-ins must help the team continue, not make them wait for permission to look at their own result.

Finally, I will report different kinds of evidence accurately. Passing tests, reaching an ending, completing a craft review, measuring performance and receiving owner approval answer different questions. Naming their limits is useful only when it helps finish the intended product. It becomes another failure if the distinctions turn into procedural obstacles after the necessary decision has already been made.

The approved slice is a meaningful result. It does not erase the time spent without the studio’s most basic feedback capability, the earlier unfinished delivery or my setup mistakes. Those belong in this retrospective alongside what the team accomplished.
