## Why This Revision Exists

V3 was previously marked 9.1/10 by the editor, but Susy rejected that approval because the first screen still feels too difficult to enter.

The issue is not research accuracy. The issue is reader arrival.

The current opening asks a general English-speaking reader to process too much too soon:

- cempasuchil;
- CONABIO/EncicloVida;
- Malinalco;
- Tonalxochitl;
- Dia de Muertos context;
- calendula / Tagetes distinctions;
- source-style caveats in the quick meaning and takeaways.

Those details matter, but they need a ramp. The article should make the reader feel, in the first few seconds: "I know what I landed on, I can picture it, and I want to keep going."

## Revision Objective

Create a V4 draft that keeps the V3 research, safety discipline, and Bloom Whispers insight while making the first screen easy, sensory, and curiosity-led.

V4 must pass this test:

```txt
Would Susy, or a general English-speaking reader with no special cultural/botanical background, keep reading after the first five seconds?
```

If the answer is no, the draft is not ready.

## Required Reader-Arrival Ramp

Use this order:

```txt
marigold -> gold/orange petals people recognize -> memory, welcome, grief, celebration, or a path -> Mexican marigold / Day of the Dead context -> cempasuchil -> source-backed Tonalxochitl detail later
```

Do not use this order:

```txt
cempasuchil -> CONABIO/EncicloVida -> Malinalco -> Tonalxochitl -> botanical/source caveat -> emotional meaning
```

## Opening Requirements

The first 100 to 150 words must:

- start with `marigold` or another familiar English anchor;
- give the reader a sensory image: color, petals, scent, path, altar, hand, doorway, table, or light;
- explain the simple article promise in plain English;
- create curiosity without forcing the reader to decode source names;
- introduce cempasuchil only after the reader understands the basic scene;
- delay CONABIO/EncicloVida, Malinalco, Tonalxochitl, and botanical Latin until later.

The opening should feel closer to the working live Bloom Whispers patterns:

- Queen Anne's Lace: lets the reader see the flower first, then reveals the strange detail.
- Spider Lily in Anime: starts with a scene going quiet, then explains the symbol.
- Hibiscus: starts with sensory force, then widens into meaning.

## What To Keep

Keep these V3 strengths:

- The central idea that marigold is "ritual gold."
- The placement-flower insight: where the gold goes changes what it means.
- The path idea: cempasuchil as a flower someone can follow.
- "Marigold is gold with memory in it."
- "Cempasuchil is the flower that makes welcome visible."
- "Marigold does not ask you to be cheerful. It asks you to place your light well."
- The distinction between Mexican cempasuchil / Tagetes and calendula / pot marigold.
- The safety note that food, herbal, recipes, oils, teas, benefits, and edible-use content are cultural/history context, not advice.

## What To Move Later

Move these out of the opening and early quick scan unless they are introduced in very plain language:

- CONABIO/EncicloVida;

- Tonalxochitl;
- Kew / institutional proof;
- botanical Latin;

These can remain in the article, source section, claims used, or the Bloom Whisper once the reader is already oriented.

## Quick Meaning Revision

The Quick Meaning should answer the reader in a warm, simple way before caveats.

It should probably begin with something like:

```txt
Marigold meaning changes with where the flower is placed: on an altar, in a garland, on a grave, in a wedding doorway, or in a pattern. Most often, marigold carries sun, remembrance, welcome, devotion, celebration, grief, and beauty used with intention.
```

Then add the plant distinction and safety note in shorter, calmer language.

Avoid opening the quick meaning with multiple unfamiliar plant names.

## Key Takeaways Revision

Limit the first key takeaways to 4 to 5 bullets. They should feel like helpful orientation, not a research checklist.

Prioritize:

- Marigold is not one meaning; it changes by plant and occasion.
- In Mexico, the Day of the Dead marigold is used for color, scent, paths, altars, memory, and welcome.
- Calendula/pot marigold is a different marigold with European grief, sun-following, herbal, and literary layers.
- The best Bloom Whispers idea: marigold is a placement flower, gold given a job.
- Safety: this is meaning/history, not medical, edible, recipe, or foraging advice.

Move the Tonalxochitl/source-specific stored-sun detail into the Bloom Whisper later.

## First H2 Revision

The first H2 should not feel like a taxonomy lecture.

Better direction:

```txt
The Simple Meaning: Marigold Is Gold Given A Job
```

or

```txt
Why Marigold Meaning Depends On Where The Gold Goes
```

Then transition into "which marigold do you mean?" after the reader has the emotional map.

## Sample Opening Direction

This is guidance, not mandatory final copy:

```txt
Marigolds are easy to recognize before they are easy to explain.

They are the gold flowers people scatter on paths, string into garlands, tuck into altars, plant in gardens, and paint into patterns. Bright enough for a celebration. Serious enough for grief.

That is why marigold meaning can feel confusing at first. The flower does not carry one simple message. Its meaning changes with where people place it.

On a Day of the Dead altar, the Mexican marigold known as cempasuchil can help make welcome visible. In an old European flower book, pot marigold can lean toward grief. In a wedding garland, marigold becomes devotion and joy.

The secret is placement: marigold is gold given a job.
```

The final V4 can be more lyrical than this, but it must stay this easy to enter.
