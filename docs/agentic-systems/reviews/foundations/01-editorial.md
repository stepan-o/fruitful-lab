## Blocking edits

### 1. Carry one promise through value and total cost

Blocks 8 and 27 do not yet meet each other. The weekly brief promises that an analyst finds a consequential contradiction earlier; the ending then lists evaluation, integration, attention, and operation. Show the actual tradeoff between them. For this explicitly hypothetical example, explain how an agent might find the contradiction sooner while making the analyst spend longer verifying its sources, and what a comparison with the current brief would need to observe. A useful early finding must survive verification and arrive while the decision remains open.

This can take one paragraph near the close, replacing generic exhortation rather than adding another section. Give the reader a concrete reason to commission or abandon the system. Keep unknown quantities unknown. The chapter needs a worked judgment about total burden, not a token disclaimer standing in for one.

### 2. Make the desk discovery do the intellectual work

Blocks 17–20 correctly name MDA, information value, and mental-model learning, but mostly leave them adjacent. The original mistake supplied the missing causal story: a failed run revealed what inspection sold, making one supposed choice inferior. Restore that admission briefly. Then show the levels: the rule made a policy dominant; that policy removed the intended decision; the intended experience therefore could not arise from the functioning buttons.

The corrected desk can then demonstrate how changing information and failure costs makes different policies sensible in different circumstances. That is substantial game-design reasoning and directly useful to someone hiring a contractor. It also restores Root’s accountable first-person voice. Currently “I am Root” and “I find” identify a narrator without revealing a judgment the narrator had to revise. Avoid repeating chapter 3’s browser-access confession; the desk mistake is distinct.

### 3. Develop Generative Agents, and distinguish a plausible choice from a failure

Blocks 23–24 improve the original purpose comparison, but the case remains a compact result followed by caveats. Give memory, retrieval/reflection, and planning one actual causal connection in the invitation story, then explain what undertaking that continuing machinery costs.

The primary paper reports that its twenty-five-agent, two-day simulation took multiple days and thousands of dollars in token credits. That dated finding could anchor the operating-cost argument, explicitly as a historical study rather than current pricing. It is better evidence than an abstract warning that more intelligence costs something. [Primary source, sections 7.1.2 and 8.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.03442v2).

Change the “forgetting an invitation” comparison. The paper distinguishes conflicting commitments from agents expressing interest but failing to plan attendance. A declined invitation can be appropriate behavior in a social simulation; a failure to plan remains an architectural limitation even if an observer can invent a plausible story around it. Use that distinction to deepen the argument rather than allowing “believable” to excuse any outcome. The same system can produce an attractive event and still fail part of its intended mechanism.

### 4. Finish the conceptual remapping of the graphics

Block 16, the opening-dialogue screenshot, is an orphan. Neither MDA nor subsequent prose examines it. Its R10 identifier, automatic-control status, timestamp, later-revision qualification, and framing preservation all reconstruct history without advancing this chapter. Remove it from chapter 1. If every existing image must remain somewhere, place it with chapter 2’s authority/information discussion and give it an interpretive role there.

Block 3 overclaims the ThreeLoops analogy. The implemented diagram has **three stations and two interfaces**, not three boundaries. Its middle station is a running world with state and guards. A generated report is an artifact; it does not automatically have that same middle loop. Frame the diagram as the distinctions this case reveals and identify the corresponding builder, delivered system/artifact, and user in another commission. Do not say that all report production instantiates the same three loops. The interactive labels still say Game and Player, so the caption should acknowledge the case terms accurately or the labels should be remapped before publication.

Block 10’s new title is promising, but the visual still defaults to checkpoint → third output → departure and retains detailed card-state readings. Give its tabs/readouts the conceptual jobs of authoritative result, readable presentation, and performance. Shorten the checkpoint/night exposition. Otherwise the old chronology survives inside an instrument that the new prose appears to have left behind.

### 5. Remove the repeated opening lesson from chapter 3

Chapter 1’s blocks 6–8 and chapter 3’s “Give autonomy a useful boundary” repeat the same Anthropic workflow/agent distinction, known route versus adaptive investigation, and the need for a worthwhile destination. Keep a short operational definition in chapter 1; chapter 3 should own choosing between a workflow, an adaptive worker, and a separate delegate. The ReAct passage can move to the feedback chapter, or remain only if it adds a concrete observation that chapter 3 does not teach again.

Do not remove the brief example itself: its recipient-level purpose is chapter 1’s necessary nongame anchor. Trim how the machinery chooses its route and develop whether its result is useful.

## Boundaries and literary flow

The opening has a persuasive plainness, especially “The recipient inherits everything that the task left out.” The title and standfirst add different information. The argument’s intended progression is now sound: recipient → local success versus useful result → experience-driven design → purpose-dependent judgment → commission.

The early agent/workflow mini-lecture delays that progression. Shortening it will make space for the two deeper case passages without lengthening the chapter. The numbered exercise is strong and genuinely transferable.

Chapter 2 now owns state, authority, retries, and attributed belief. Chapter 1 should not explain those mechanisms beyond establishing that they impose obligations on the promise. Chapter 3 owns organizational boundaries and the cost of context transfer. Its review-attention argument should develop, rather than repeat, chapter 1’s full-cost framing.

One adjacent editing defect surfaced during the boundary check: staged chapter 2 blocks 7 and 8 repeat the F.E.A.R. introduction almost point for point. Delete or merge one. This is independent of chapter 1’s revisions.

**Acceptance condition:** the reader should finish chapter 1 able to name a beneficial user change, distinguish its evidence from a local success score, and weigh the work it removes against the work it creates. The draft now names those abilities; the blocking revisions should let the reader watch someone exercise them.
