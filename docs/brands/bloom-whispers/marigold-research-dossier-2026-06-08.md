- Kew Plants of the World Online, *Calendula officinalis* L. record/general information checked as separate marigold layer: https://powo.science.kew.org/taxon/urn:lsid:ipni.org:names:187894-1/general-information

What the research found:

- *Tagetes erecta* is the main cempasúchil / Mexican marigold anchor. Kew accepts the species and gives Mexico to Guatemala as the native range.
- Kew describes *Tagetes erecta* as an annual in Asteraceae, growing mainly in the subtropical biome, with social, food, environmental, animal-food, poison, medicine, and medicinal-use data attached in useful-plant databases.
- CONABIO/EncicloVida gives a Mexico-centered description: cempasúchil is native to Mexico/Central America, wild or escaped in many Mexican states, and associated with a long domesticating/cultural history.
- CONABIO notes the plant has aromatic stems/leaves, capitula/head-like inflorescences, flowers from yellow to red-orange, and many disk flowers in simple heads or ligule transformations in "double" heads.
- The UCL/JTCM PDF is the strongest science-meets-history source. It frames *Tagetes erecta* as a Mexican species whose roles changed from Mesoamerican ritual/medicinal plant to global ornamental, industrial, lutein, and supplement/feed resource.
- The UCL paper is useful but needs careful editorial handling: it discusses modern biomedical and pharmacological research, but Bloom Whispers should not turn this into supplement promotion.
- The ASyD PDF adds an ethnoagronomy angle: cultivation timing matters culturally, because traditional planting and flowering schedules were adapted to deliver flowers at the end of October for Día de Muertos in a syncretic context.
- Calendula/pot marigold is a separate plant. The Shakespeare, European herbalism, and many floriography claims are about calendula/pot marigold, not cempasúchil. This should become an early "which marigold?" box.

Strongest usable findings:

- Marigold is a name-confusion flower: *Tagetes* and *Calendula* both matter, but they do not carry the same evidence.
- The "gold" in cempasúchil is not just color. Sources repeatedly tie the flower to sun, warmth, aroma, and return.
- *Tagetes erecta* has a modern industrial/scientific afterlife through carotenoids/lutein, but that belongs in a historical/science box, not a benefits pitch.

Weak or unverified material:

- Do not state "marigold is native to Africa" because African marigold is a horticultural common name; *Tagetes erecta* is Mexican/Central American in the sources.
- Do not state all marigolds are edible or medicinal.
- Do not merge calendula skincare/herbal claims into cempasúchil unless a source specifically supports that species and usage.

- H3: "Cempasúchil vs Calendula vs French/African Marigold"
- Source-backed callout: "African marigold is a common name, not an African origin story."

### Lane 2. Folklore / Tradition

Sources checked/read:

- INAH, "Cempasúchil y mariposas: símbolos del regreso de las almas": https://www.inah.gob.mx/index.php/foto-del-dia/cempasuchil-y-mariposas-simbolos-del-regreso-de-las-almas
- CONABIO/EncicloVida PDF, "Cempasúchil (*Tagetes erecta*)": https://enciclovida.mx/especies/180679.pdf
- Serrato-Cruz, ASyD PDF: https://www.revista-asyd.org/index.php/asyd/article/download/1339/716
- UCL/JTCM PDF: https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10203572/1/1-s2.0-S2225411024000920-main.pdf
- INAH "Rompecabezas Cempasúchil" source surfaced in search as an institutional clue, but not used as primary proof because it was an interactive puzzle page rather than a deeper article.

What the research found:

- INAH gives the clearest reader-facing folklore/tradition line: during Día de Muertos, cempasúchil petals are scattered on paths/offerings to guide souls home, while aroma guides their arrival.
- INAH ties cempasúchil to the sun and renewal of life from pre-Hispanic times, and connects butterflies/mariposas to death, regeneration, and the essence of the dead in Mesoamerican symbolism.
- INAH also says different *Tagetes* species are used across regions for tombs, paths, patios, and altars. This helps avoid one-species simplification.
- CONABIO/EncicloVida gives a striking cultural detail from Malinalco: small yellow flowers called Tonalxochitl were believed to preserve the warmth of solar rays in their corollas. This is a strong Bloom Reveal support if worded carefully as a reported belief.
- ASyD deepens the tradition beyond "the flower guides the dead." It says current cultivation and use reflect syncretism: pre-Hispanic planting/flower-use calendars were adapted to the Catholic All Saints/Day of the Dead timing.
- ASyD also describes community altar/graveyard practices: cempoalxóchitl flowers, other flowers, candles, holy water, prayers, copal smoke, food, and the emotional atmosphere of return.
- UCL adds that the Florentine Codex describes cempasúchil flower heads as ceremonial objects held by participants, garlands adorning neck/head, and symbolic stones in ritual contexts. This makes the flower older and more ceremonial than a modern altar decoration.

Strongest usable findings:

- Bloom Reveal candidate: cempasúchil is a flower of return, not merely a decoration. Its color, scent, and seasonal timing create a ritual environment for the dead to be welcomed home.
- The solar/corolla warmth idea from CONABIO is memorable and magical-feeling, but source-backed.
- The ASyD calendar/cultivation angle is unusually strong: the ritual meaning is not only in the flower, but also in how people time, plant, protect, harvest, and use it.

Weak or unverified material:

- Avoid "Aztecs used cempasúchil exactly the same way modern Mexicans do on November 1 and 2." ASyD explicitly complicates that.
- Avoid using romanticized "ancient Aztec legend" versions unless sourced from a reliable cultural or academic source.

Content opportunity:

- H2: "Why Cempasúchil Is the Flower of Día de Muertos"
- H3: "Color, Scent, Paths, and the Return of the Dead"
- H3: "A Syncretic Flower, Not a Simple Survival"

Paid/library need:

- Optional. Books or journal articles on regional Día de Muertos practice, Arqueología Mexicana issues, and ethnographies of Oaxaca/Puebla/Michoacán could add story texture.

### Lane 3. Symbolism / Flower Meaning

Sources checked/read:

- INAH cempasúchil/mariposas article: https://www.inah.gob.mx/index.php/foto-del-dia/cempasuchil-y-mariposas-simbolos-del-regreso-de-las-almas
- CONABIO/EncicloVida PDF: https://enciclovida.mx/especies/180679.pdf
- UCL/JTCM PDF: https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10203572/1/1-s2.0-S2225411024000920-main.pdf
- Henrietta Dumont, *The Language of Flowers* / *The Floral Offering*, Project Gutenberg: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/71779/pg71779-images.html
- Kate Greenaway, *Language of Flowers*, Project Gutenberg: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/31591/pg31591-images.html
- Shakespeare Birthplace Trust, "Shakespeare's Favourite Flowers: The Marigold": https://www.shakespeare.org.uk/explore-shakespeare/blogs/marigold-on-death-beds-blowing-the-marigold-in-shakespeare-and-victorian-england/

What the research found:

- For Mexican cempasúchil, the strongest meanings are return, memory, sun, life/death cycle, scent-guidance, renewal, graves, altars, paths, and connection between natural and spiritual worlds.
- INAH is especially useful for a concise meaning statement: cempasúchil represents a connection between natural and spiritual worlds and reminds readers that life and death are part of the same cycle.
- For European/pot marigold, older floriography and Shakespearean material can support grief, distress, remembrance, sun-following, opening/closing with the sun, and grave/tomb association.
- Dumont states that marigold alone expresses grief and with other flowers represents life's mixed events. This is likely calendula/pot marigold, not Mexican cempasúchil.
