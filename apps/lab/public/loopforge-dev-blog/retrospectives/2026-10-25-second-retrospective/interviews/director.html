Scene Director — second interview

Fresh answers for the approved R10 milestone.

Q1

What is the most useful result you personally contributed this cycle? Describe the player/team outcome, what you actually inspected, and the evidence that could contradict your assessment.

I helped turn the opening into one shared sequence rather than separate camera and motion suggestions. The useful part is the difference between sam helping bob and ed taking the card. In the actual sampled frames, sam crouches beside bob while ed bends, lifts the pale card and steps away. Those are two readable choices by different people. I inspected the full-size frames at video times 74.4, 75.6, 76.4 and 81.2 seconds, not just the storyboard. The result is incomplete: bob still reads bent upright. A continuous contact review could also contradict the apparent grip or prove that bob could see ed. I would keep the distinction and repair the bodily evidence.

Q2

Which decision of your own worked poorly? Give one concrete instance, its consequence, what you knew then, and what you would change. Separate your responsibility from dependencies outside your control.

I retained the broad Conveyor view for too many interpersonal turns in D3. It was a defensible geography shot, but I let that reason carry over into exchanges that needed a readable recipient. The actual pair assignment has small bodies and a speech panel over a head; the export also shows several intended replies never reached the named listener. I knew the source had not had an integrated visual pass. I should have required one ordinary-size exchange with its real hearing record before treating the shot map as settled. Tool access was a dependency; the breadth of my provisional decision was mine.

Q3

Describe one cross-role exchange that improved the game and one that created avoidable delay or rework. What made the difference? Name the work and evidence, not just the participants.

The D3 shutdown exchange improved through a concrete Engine–Director handoff. Engine identified that mae and kit could not reach STILETTO from their posts. I required a real listening approach instead of a larger hearing radius. The R10 export now records both reports reaching her. That is a modest, verifiable improvement. By contrast, camera proposals moved between Director and Level before we had a stable picture area. We spent source work on lenses while the live interface changed the scene height whenever speech appeared. D6 now fixes that shared interface first, preserves the room and control roots, and tests the camera against it.

Q4

What did actual integrated footage or play reveal that source checks or isolated craft work did not? If you have not inspected moving evidence, say so and distinguish still-frame judgment from motion judgment.

Actual captured frames revealed heads hidden by speech, bob cropped at the Wash and Forge instructions, and the facade covering almost the entire exit view. Timed samples also show an arms-out worker translating across several seconds, rather than merely passing through one awkward pose. I inspected decoded sequences and selected full-size frames from the actual videos; I did not play an uninterrupted film. I can judge the sampled persistence and obstruction, not every blend, contact or frame-time cost. Source checks established completed workpieces and departures. They did not establish that the player could see the actions clearly.

Q5

The first retrospective called for one owned outcome, decisive evidence and a change-of-approach decision after a failed assumption. What concrete behavior in this cycle followed or missed that lesson? What should good work in your domain mean now?

We followed the lesson when the question became “did STILETTO actually receive the shutdown reports?” One owner could integrate a specific approach and the next export could answer it. We missed it when source packets and repeated staging proposals accumulated faster than actual views. I also missed earlier budget checkpoints; the later unlimited allowance does not erase those overruns. Good direction now means a specific person does something readable, another person receives it when required, and the consequence survives the next cut. A numbered shot list is preparation, not that outcome.

Q6

Choose one improvement you would keep for the next cycle. Who owns it, when should it happen, and what observable result would show it helped? Include any tradeoff or disagreement with the current production approach.

I would keep one early integrated beat review, owned by Director and supplied by Engine, before expanding a new scene. Use the ordinary viewport, final speech treatment and actual bodies, then inspect the complete action and the receiving exchange. The observable result is that a reviewer can identify the cause, recipient and consequence without a debug explanation, and the event record agrees. This costs an early capture and may slow parallel source authoring. I prefer that tradeoff to several cheap-looking source passes that later collide at the same camera, body or interface boundary.

Q7

Which intended emotional or received beat survives in actual performance, and which does not? Where did source staging or shot approval promise more than the camera/motion delivered?

sam’s attention and ed’s separate pickup survive at the level of observable sampled action. The final bob–sam exchange also survives in the hearing record: each receives the other’s line. I cannot yet certify its full emotional performance. The fall does not land as intended in the inspected samples: bob stays bent upright while sam crouches, weakening both the injury and the reason bob misses the theft. Recruitment also overpromises received conversation when max’s reply has no LIMEN receipt. The owner has approved the R10 slice; these are concrete corrections to carry forward, not a request to reopen that judgment.
