# Writing Loopforge

Editorial direction · Revision 2 · 20 October 2026

Loopforge takes place among people who have been built for work and have developed lives around it. Some want to do a good job. Some want credit, company or a quiet shift. Their supervisors can turn an ordinary need into an offense, then fill in the paperwork with complete conviction. The writing should let us enjoy knowing these people while taking what happens to them seriously.

The voice needs a good eye for how work feels: the colleague who volunteers too quickly, the expert who cannot accept help, the superior who mistakes a frightened room for a well-run one. Its humor can be dry, and its circumstances absurd, without making every character deliver a joke. Affection grows through noticing what somebody does when there is little advantage in doing it.

This revision guides the [dramaturgy primer](CHARACTER_DRAMATURGY.md), [reference studies](NARRATIVE_STUDIES.md), [cast](OPENING_CAST.md) and [opening episode](OPENING_EPISODE.md). These are design proposals, not a description of behavior already installed in the game.

## What Sanctuary teaches us about the sentence

The first two Sanctuary Economics chapters make a large subject approachable by keeping track of who is doing what. Al Alcorn has to move a cabinet to reach its coins. A console maker needs games that make its equipment worth buying. The explanation grows out of those actions. By the time a business term appears, the reader has encountered the relationship it names.

That is the craft to carry into Loopforge. A statement about authority becomes easier to understand when ed tells sam to wait beside an injured colleague. A statement about identity becomes more interesting when bob declines an offered arm. Start with enough of the situation for the reader to follow, then develop what the action means. There is no need to make every section begin with a miniature scene, but an abstraction should earn its place by explaining something we can picture.

Sanctuary also lets a detail change meaning as the argument develops. A broken machine turns out to have taken too much money; a game becomes a reason to buy an entire console. Loopforge has its own reversals. The keenest worker becomes afraid to move. The person who asks everybody to keep the line clear causes the delay. Let the circumstances do the work before adding a clever sentence about them.

The reference is a standard of clarity and development. Loopforge has a different subject, more room for dialogue and a more intimate interest in its characters. Its prose need not reproduce Sanctuary's sentences, examples or chapter structure.

## Keep a person at the center

bob is the protagonist. The player runs the factory; bob lives in it. A design passage should keep that difference clear. The player can want output while bob wants to finish a task beside someone whose respect matters to him. Neither position exhausts the story. We return to bob often enough to recognize changes in his life, including changes that began while the camera was elsewhere.

An ordinary life needs ordinary pleasures. Let bob like a task, make an unnecessary improvement, enjoy someone's company or be pleased with a small success before asking the audience to fear for him. Do not introduce him solely to be injured. His decisions must matter to other people too: he can help, disappoint, withhold, compete and make amends. Centrality gives him sustained attention, not automatic moral authority.

When the cast grows, keep one foreground question in a scene and allow other lives at its edges. Ten people do not need ten introductory speeches. A familiar gesture can bring somebody back into focus; a changed gesture can make us curious. A person's absence becomes meaningful only after their presence has become familiar.

## Write the review as an argument, not an inventory of influences

For each reference, identify a concrete scene or passage, explain how its dramatic effect is made, then state what Loopforge can use and what changes in the transfer. Connect those observations into an argument about the game we are making. Do not substitute an adjective—epic, profound, human, cinematic—for that explanation.

Separate the work, its author's stated intention and our interpretation. Cite the actual chapter, official episode description, interview or observed footage beside the claim. Mark spoilers. A synopsis can verify a premise; it cannot substantiate a shot-by-shot analysis of an episode we have not inspected. Selected passages support a focused study, not a claim to have reread an entire body of work.

Give each reference its own difficulty. Back to the Dawn makes a large social world approachable around a practical goal. Attack on Titan lets evolving loyalties alter the meaning and direction of its central conflict. Dostoevsky presses a person's account of themselves against other people who will not fit it. Explain where these methods disagree as well as where they cooperate. Loopforge needs a synthesis with its own scale and tone.

## Let characters exceed their descriptions

An archetype is a way into a person. It is not their final diagnosis. Write a competence, a pleasure and a contradiction before a list of triggers. The helper may enjoy being needed too much; the rule-follower may be trying to protect someone; the injured protagonist may treat another worker unfairly. Give their conduct a history without inventing a tragic origin for every habit.

Avoid ten portraits with the same hidden wound and ten equally eloquent lines. Some people are direct. Some cannot explain themselves. Some know exactly what they are doing and choose an excuse because it works on their listener. Leave room for kindness with no ulterior motive, and for mistakes that do not reveal a secret evil.

The strongest detail often changes meaning through use. bob's readiness can be generosity, competition or concealment of pain, depending on the occasion. Keep the action recognizable while allowing the motive and cost to develop. This gives the prose something to investigate and the actor something to perform.

## An essay needs to take the reader somewhere

The design pages are written for someone imagining the game with us. They should explain why a scene is worth making and how its parts depend on one another. A paragraph about bob's hesitation should lead naturally to the problem that hesitation causes at work. From there, we can consider the player's response and what the next encounter might reveal.

Avoid describing the result we want without giving the reader the experience: recognizable characters, consequential choices, readable reactions. Those words have uses in review criteria. Repeating them in the essay merely postpones the explanation. Name the person, the action and the consequence instead. Keep technical requirements in the production section where a builder can use them.

Connected prose also needs a little room. A paragraph can establish a situation, complicate it and explain what changed. Short sentences can mark a pause or a reversal; a continuous run of them makes every observation sound like a slogan. Read the passage aloud. Where the voice seems to be announcing important principles one after another, restore the ordinary relationship between the sentences.

A heading should help a reader return to the passage. A caption should identify a source or draw attention to something the image makes available. Neither needs to repeat the paragraph's conclusion. State proposal status and necessary limits once, where they affect the reader's understanding; the essay should not repeatedly interrupt itself to discuss its own production.

## Follow the pressure through people

Pressure is one of Loopforge’s central themes. Give it a source, a route and a particular recipient. A quota becomes a demand; a demand becomes bob’s promise; keeping the promise can become a reason to conceal pain. That chain is more revealing than saying the factory is stressful. It also makes a possible interruption visible: someone can clarify a request, accept a delay or refuse an additional task.

Pressure can come from affection, gratitude, pride or the fear of disappointing a colleague. Allow relief, pleasure and successful work to have consequences too. Do not use intensity as a substitute for development or let every warm moment serve only as preparation for punishment.

## Names carry rank and acquire a history

The test for the writing is the difference between “STILETTO hit a robot worker” and “STILETTO hit bob.” The player has to know enough about bob for the second sentence to mean more. The name should recall something bob did, someone who knows him or an expectation the incident has interrupted. Use repeated encounters to build that familiarity before relying on it for a dramatic payoff.

All workers use short, single-syllable human names in lowercase. The opening cast is bob, sam, ed, jo, gus, max, mae, kit, lou and ben. Preserve the lowercase spelling at the beginning of a sentence, in a heading, on a speaker label and in a caption. Supervisor names remain uppercase. Do not apply an uppercase visual treatment to every speaker label or automatically title-case a worker name; either would erase the hierarchy.

Worker IDs belong to production records and appropriate in-world paperwork. A scene can show LIMEN reducing a person to a number, but the surrounding writing should help the player keep hold of who that person is. These names replace the earlier workshop nicknames; they do not describe personality classes or assign a worker to a fixed behavior.

## The factory has more than one way of speaking

The factory's official language makes judgments sound like descriptions. Someone is cleared, serviceable, available or below target. The speaker may believe the label is accurate. Its limits become visible when an injured worker is cleared for entry or a supposedly available colleague is already carrying too much.

Workers use much of the same vocabulary because it describes their lives. They also shorten it, resent it, joke about it and ask one another what it means. A worker should be able to say “That hurt” without translating pain into a diagnostic message. “I can finish the shift” may reveal more about a damaged joint than a fault code would.

Supervisors each have a preferred account of the factory. LIMEN notices breaches and permissions. STILETTO notices work waiting to happen and time being wasted. CATHEXIS notices the quality of attention she is receiving, the pleasures available to her and the indignities of the task. Their vocabularies will overlap. Their priorities, the person they are addressing and how much patience they have left should make the difference.

The player's controls need a more straightforward voice. Instructions, costs and outcomes must be understandable even when the fiction around them is evasive. Flavor can accompany a choice, but it must not conceal what the player is choosing. The writing in a briefing belongs to its speaker; a control label is our responsibility.

## A line is something one person does to another

Before writing a speech bubble, know who is being addressed and what the speaker hopes the line will accomplish. sam offers help. bob refuses the appearance of needing it. ed warns sam, and also reassures himself that the rule still applies. A line that reports a character's entire motivation is usually less convincing than one that tries to get something done.

Consider this small voice study after the fall:

**sam:** Here. Lean on me.

**bob:** I can walk.

**sam:** All right. I'll stay on this side.

sam lets the refusal stand and walks close enough to be useful. There is concern in the exchange, but neither worker has to name it. bob retains a little dignity. Whether sam can sustain that restraint in a later scene remains interesting.

Brief dialogue can still have rhythm. A speaker may answer the wrong part of a question, wait too long or revise a claim halfway through. Leave room for the body to complete the thought. Spread a longer statement across bubbles when the speaker has a reason to pause or change emphasis, rather than chopping a paragraph into arbitrary lengths. Use an emotion mark or emphatic word when it adds a performance cue the player might otherwise miss; repeated shouting and a permanent parade of emojis flatten the differences between lines.

## Different speakers need different pressures

LIMEN can explain himself patiently. His frightening quality comes partly from how little doubt he has about his right to decide. He will sometimes offer reassurance that is perfectly consistent with his priorities and useless to the person receiving it. Keep explosive commands for the moments when control appears to slip; the quiet return to procedure can carry the aftermath.

STILETTO wants output, speed and leadership’s approval. Workers really are held up, the line really is idle, and the quota has not become smaller while LIMEN checks another card. Her objection is that useful labor is being withheld. Do not write it as concern for the workers’ wellbeing or safety. Protecting a productive resource can motivate an action without making that action compassionate. Her language can move quickly toward a demand. She should also be able to persuade, praise skill or make an attractive promise; otherwise her impatience will have nowhere to build.

CATHEXIS enjoys an audience and knows how to address one. She can invite a worker into a private joke, turn a complaint into a small performance or fall abruptly out of charm when the job becomes coarse and filthy. Theatrical does not mean ornate in every line. A simple request, allowed to hang until somebody complies, may be more effective than a speech.

A short proposed exchange about the empty Conveyor helps separate the first two voices:

**STILETTO:** I've got a whole line waiting for ten workers.

**LIMEN:** You'll have them when they're cleared.

**STILETTO:** Then clear them. The quota doesn't get smaller while we stand here.

**LIMEN:** Neither does my responsibility for who goes through this door.

They are arguing about the same delay and defending different duties. Neither needs to announce a personality trait. Later lines can become more personal because the practical disagreement is already understood.

The workers need comparable differences without ten verbal gimmicks. bob offers more than he has been asked for. ed seeks an exact instruction. jo is careful about what they can claim. lou pays attention to what their listener wants to hear. ben may say very little, but an answer should still be an answer. Give each room to become tired, playful, evasive or unexpectedly direct; a habitual voice should survive those changes without making every scene sound the same.

## Humor has to belong to somebody

There is comedy in waiting for permission to begin work while the time left to meet the quota keeps shrinking. There is comedy in a supervisor calling an orderly room a success after personally injuring one of its occupants. We do not need an accompanying joke to make the contradiction available to the audience.

max may joke because they want somebody beside them to relax. CATHEXIS may make an elegant complaint because she wants witnesses. A worker can misuse official wording quite deliberately to spare a friend. These jokes have an audience and a purpose inside the world. Some will fail. The pause after a failed joke can tell us who no longer feels safe enough to laugh.

Avoid turning the injury itself into a sight gag. Once bob has been hit, the recovery needs time and the damaged leg needs to keep mattering. Humor can return through the awkwardness of accepting help or the institution's determination to finish its form. Cruelty becomes harder to take seriously if the scene immediately rewards it with a punchline at the victim's expense.

Warmth deserves the same restraint. ben can save a place without the prose declaring an act of solidarity. A returned tool, a remembered preference or a task done carefully may be enough. Let the player have some of the pleasure of noticing.

## Mechanical words need mechanical lives

Use the factory's materials where they are useful: enamel worn at a grip, a joint that catches on the second step, a card held too tightly, a clean plate someone takes pride in. Select the detail that affects the encounter. A catalogue of pistons and brass adds little when the reader needs to know why a worker will not hand over a part.

Words such as useful, spare, worn, cleared and fit can carry human implications without being announced as metaphors. A request for an inspection may be sensible, intrusive or threatening depending on who makes it and who can refuse. The fiction earns that range through actual uses of inspection and repair. Invented diagnostics and constant substitutions of “circuit” for “feeling” would make the workers less individual.

Preserve the world's knowledge limits. A character can misunderstand, lie or repeat gossip, but must have some route to what they say. Do not casually introduce knowledge of the concealed factory purpose, a new origin story or an unapproved ability for the sake of a neat line. Their immediate concerns provide plenty to talk about.

## The next editorial pass

Read an entire scene or section before repairing individual sentences. Check that it develops rather than repeating its premise, and that the people still want understandable things. Remove explanations the action already supplies. Restore context where a striking line has become too compressed to understand.

For dialogue, cover the names and read the exchange with its actions. The voices need not be identifiable from every isolated sentence, but the conversation should reveal who is trying to achieve what. Then read it with the animation: keep the words that change our understanding, and allow silence where the body already finishes the thought.

Review the foundation, studies, portraits and script against the same standard. A portrait should make a person imaginable; the script should then put that person in a situation the portrait cannot settle in advance. Keep observations, design decisions, proposed outcomes and implementation status distinct.
